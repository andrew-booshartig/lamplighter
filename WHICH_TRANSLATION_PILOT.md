# Which translation? The Hermetic Museum

The pilot for Q1 and Q4 of the quality pass (`ROADMAP.md`, "The quality pass"), written 18 November 2026. Part 1 is the card as it would stand in the reader, in the style of the High-control groups cards: what exists, how the versions differ, what is said about them with its grade, which edition the Library holds and why, and what it does not hold. Part 2 is how the answer was found, with the numbers, so the same steps can be run for the next book. The card is written in the tool's neutral voice: no verdict, every claim about a person or a judgement attributed, and "not found" is not "not so".

Grades, as everywhere in the Library: **documented** (a record anyone can check: a title page, a preface, a catalogue record), **reported or claimed** (said by someone; named), **disputed** (sources disagree; both shown).

## Part 1. The card

### The Hermetic Museum (*Musaeum Hermeticum*): which translation?

**The work.** A collection of twenty-two alchemical tracts, published in Latin at Frankfurt in 1678 as the enlarged form of an earlier collection of 1625 (documented: the 1893 title page and preface). Waite's preface calls the 1625 collection scarcer and "of less value": that is his judgement. Wikipedia says the 1625 collection was first published in German and that the 1678 Latin edition was reprinted in 1749; its own text is inconsistent about the language of 1625, and neither point was checked against a primary source (reported).

**What exists.**

*A book with the same words in its title, Alexander Roob's* Alchemy & Mysticism: The Hermetic Museum *(Taschen, 2014), is a different work; it is not a translation of this collection.*

**How the 1893 English differs.**
- It is a version "from the Latin". For the tracts first written in another language the English reader has a translation of a translation: documented for the title page; reported for Norton's *Ordinal*, whose Latin form in the collection is said to be Michael Maier's, first printed in 1618. Which other tracts were first written in German or English was not checked.
- Its editor describes it as giving "both the spirit and the sense of the original without the original's prolixity" (documented: Waite's preface; his own description). That is a sense-for-sense rendering, with some cutting of repetition, by the editor's account. How much was cut was not measured here.
- A personal alchemy-history site (Iulia Millesima, LabyrinthDesigners, 2010, updated 2023) compares the Latin *Introitus apertus* (Amsterdam, 1667) with the 1893 *Open Entrance* and counts omissions and inaccuracies, giving sentences of the Latin that the English leaves out (claimed: one author, one tract, not peer reviewed; the examples can be checked against the two texts). The same page says the 1669 English of William Cooper was made from a different source and is a different text.
- No scholarly review of the accuracy of the 1893 English, tract by tract, was found in the sources opened (a search of catalogues, Wikipedia and the web for reviews and for named historians of alchemy). That is a gap in what was looked at, not a finding.

**Which one the Library holds, and why.** The 1893 English, as a typed copy with its printed page numbers (volume and page). Reason, a labelled editorial choice and not a verdict: it is the only complete English version found, it is in the public domain, and the later reprints repeat it. The Library's first copy was the machine-read text of a scan; it was replaced on 18 November 2026 (Part 2 has the reasons). The Library also holds the Open Entrance on its own (`h-open`: the same 1893 text, 99.5% of its words identical to this copy's).

**What the Library does not hold.** The Latin of 1678 (a Latin text; the scan addresses are in Part 2). Modern editions and translations (in copyright). The single-tract English versions of 1650 and 1669 (public domain; not fetched). The engraved frontispieces and plates of the 1893 volumes.

**What is still imperfect in the Library's copy.** A few typing slips remain (for example "he" for "be": about a hundred places in 231,000 words are marked as likely slips, which a later review of the typed copy against the scans can mend). The explanation of the frontispiece emblems and the printed list of the Open Entrance's chapters are in the scans and not in the typed copy (about 600 words). Pages with no mark: the plates and title pages, and two pages of text.

**To check for yourself.** Compare a passage with the page images: the original files card for this text links the two volumes of the printed edition at the Internet Archive; the Latin is at the Internet Archive (`musaeumhermeticu00meri`) and at the Science History Institute.

## Part 2. How the answer was found

### What "more accurate" could mean, and what was found
(a) A cleaner text of the same edition. **A better scan: none.** Two scans of the 1893 volumes exist besides a Google Books copy of volume 1 (a 1978 microfilm facsimile). Read by the Library's own tools after the same clean-up, the Glasgow scan is no cleaner than the one the Library had; the Google scan is worse. The two full scans misread the italic type in the same way. **A typed copy: yes**, a complete one. (b) A different public-domain translation of the same work: none of the whole; single tracts as in the card. (c) Modern translations in copyright: none of the whole found.

Thirty passages of the Library's text (the named one, "Metals have their own seed", and 29 drawn from paragraph starts across both volumes, seed 18), set against the same words in each candidate: word list, unknown words 3.26% (Library), 3.24% against 3.11% on the same passages (Glasgow), 2.83% (typed); stray tokens 0.17% (Library), 0.04% (typed); differences from the typed copy per 1,000 words: Library 11.0, Glasgow 11.4, raw scan text 20.7, Google 29.0 (volume 1 only). Thirty passages cannot separate the sources by the word list (the floor above); the whole-book rows can. In the named passage both scans give "z¢s earth" or "z/s earth" and "ove thing" or "oze thing"; the typed copy gives "its earth" and "one thing".

How good is the typed copy? Two scans that agree with each other and differ from it mark where it may be wrong: 466 places in 231,696 words; 72 are line-break or ligature differences, and of the other 394 most are the scans' own shared misreadings (italic "i.e." read as "ze", "z", "1" or "l" 46 times, words run together 105 times, "etc." read as "c"). About 110 look like typing slips ("he irrigated" for "be irrigated", "geld" for "gold", "sheaved" for "shewed", "riot" for "not"), about 0.05% of the words at most. Spelling is the print's ("shewn", "Phœnix"), not tidied.

### Same edition, and the pages
The typed copy and the Library's scan text agree word for word on 98% of words; the 5-word anchors cover 98.9% of the typed text. All the tracts of both volumes are in it, in the 1893 order. The printed page numbers were found from the scans, not typed: the Library's matcher against the first scan (579 pages), the same pages found by first words (28), the Glasgow scan's page data for volume 1 (24), and the Glasgow text's own running heads for the four pages the first scan lacks (4): 635 marks, 343 in volume 1 and 292 in volume 2. Where both scans mark the same page they agree within a paragraph for 274 of 281 pages (253 to the same word); the other seven differ by a heading line, except one where the Glasgow head was garbled. Pages are 413 words long at the median, none longer than 474.

### The swap and the files
Done through the Library's own writers: `tools/texts_hmuseum.py` (new; `build`, `marks`, `check`), called by `build_texts.py` after the other typed texts, so a full rebuild gives the same text; `texts/h-museum.js`, its entry in `texts/index.js` (only that entry changed), `texts/LICENSES.txt` (one line), `texts/pg-h-museum.js`, `texts/pgs.js` (one line). `texts/originals.js` is unchanged and still right: the page finder and the original-files card point at the printed edition, because the scan spec stays in `tools/texts_shelf.py`. Guarded by `tests/hmuseum.test.js` (28 checks). The old files are kept in the session's scratchpad until the project manager confirms. The entry's note and licence must not contain the words "scan" or "OCR": the reader and the Random page take a text for machine-read when they do.

### For the next book (Q1)
1. List the candidates from the Archive's own search and item records (title, creator, OCR engine, number of images, rights), and from Gutenberg's catalogue, Wikisource and Sacred Texts (the last may refuse).
2. Read the pages first: in each scan's page-number data, a printed number that jumps by more than the leaves do means missing pages. One scan of this book lacked four pages and another three, in the same tract.
3. Run the word list and the stray-token test, but expect a floor in a specialist book, and expect italic type to be misread by scans of one printing (here both full scans did, in the same way); a second scan rarely helps, a typed copy does.
4. Where a typed copy exists, align it with each scan and count; two scans agreeing against it list its slips for the corrections layer (Q2). The list of 394 for this book is in the shared notes folder.
5. Rebuild the page marks from the scans, never by guessing, and fill gaps from a second scan only where the first words of a page are found once in the place the pages around it allow.
6. Say in the entry's note what the copy is and what is imperfect, in words that do not trip the machine-read test; add a test for the book.
