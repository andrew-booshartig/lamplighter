# The Lamplighter Project

*Formerly called Beatlab. Short form: Lamplighter.*

A free, offline place to study human religion, to worship and study with others, and to sit in stillness, with a tone lab for binaural-beat experiments. Every claim is graded for its evidence, self-tests are blinded, and nothing leaves your browser.
One self-contained file, no install, no network: **`Lamplighter.html`** (next to this folder; `build.py` also writes the same file to `dist/`) — double-click it (Chrome, Edge, Safari, Firefox). The Library's full texts live in a **`texts/`** folder next to it (see "The text library" below); keep the two together. Tip: use the same browser each time, and export a backup from Setup now and then — your data lives in that browser.

## How the app is organised (reorganised 16 November 2026, made simpler on 18 November 2026)

The maker asked for an interface where nobody gets lost while studying ("K.I.S.S."), quiet and content first, in the manner of the Zed editor. The app now has **six places in the menu and a small gear**: **Timeline** (where the app opens), **Traditions**, **Library**, **Random** (a place of its own since 19 November 2026), **Notebook** and **Practice**, with **Setup** as the gear at the foot of the menu (the last item of the bar on a phone, where the six places and the gear fit in one row at 375 px, 320 px too). Each place has one row of **at most four tabs**, and a tab that holds several views has a second row of small chips. The pages that are kept but are not first choices sit under that place's **More**, a menu at the end of the row (Bibles side by side, the text catalogue, the Glossary and Your own texts under Library; the sound overview under Practice; the old Start page, now called Find your way, under Timeline): nothing was removed, and the `/` search finds every one of them by what a person would type. **Worship together** (the Gather pages, `#/gather`) is in no menu at all, at the maker's request ("hide it from the menu, keep the code, keep the information on how things are practised"): it is found by the `/` search, by its address, and by one line on Practice → Ways to worship ("Worship together: how groups meet and keep time"); in the sections table it is a tab marked `hidden`, and its own pages name themselves in their title. An empty or unknown address opens History. Every page is under exactly one tab (`tests/sections.test.js` checks that nothing is missing and nothing is twice), and every address (`#/path/library`, `#/atlas/calendars`, `#/meditate`, `#/start`, `#/gather` …) is the same as before, so saved links and bookmarks still open. ### Belief: four ways to find a tradition (and the Library arranged by them)

`src/js/2z-belief-core.js` sorts the 793 traditions of the Atlas and the 40 shelves of the Library under **One God (monotheism)**, **Many gods (polytheism)**, **Spirit in all things (animism)** and **Inner paths and philosophy (spirituality)**, with the deep past (archaeological horizons) and, in the Library, *About religion* and the imagined shelf in their own places. It is a way to find things, not a verdict, and every page that shows a label says so: a tradition has a first label and the others it is also described by (Hinduism is many gods, also inner paths; Daoism inner paths, also many gods; Sufism one God, also inner paths; Yoruba religion many gods, also spirit), and each belief carries a line about where its label is not clean. The rules: a tradition's own entry; otherwise a new religious movement out of a tradition of many gods or of spirits is an inner path; otherwise the label of the tradition it grew from (the Atlas's parent link); otherwise the label of its family. **Traditions → Browse by belief** opens each belief with its best known traditions, the shelves of the Library that hold their texts, and the whole list (`#/atlas/traditions?b=one`, filtered, with a Belief column); **Texts → Library** now opens arranged by belief (six places, each shelf once, a shelf that belongs under two ideas says so), with a switch to the older *Region and era* collections (`?a=collection`; the choice is remembered). Tests: `tests/belief.test.js`.

### All traditions: an overview, filters and a list (18 November 2026)

Traditions → Browse → **All traditions** (`#/atlas/traditions`, the same address as before, so every older link still opens; `src/js/2z-tradlist-core.js` holds the rules, `6zza-tradlist.js` draws the page, `tests/tradlist.test.js` checks both; the old table in `6q-atlas-pages.js` is gone and that file only calls the new page). The maker said of the old page: “there should be a good way to organize these, and to filter them”. It now opens on a **short overview**, not a wall of 793: the Atlas’s nine groups of families (the deep past; the ancient Near East and Mediterranean; Abrahamic; Iranian; Indian and Himalayan; East, Inner and Southeast Asian; African and Afro-diasporic; Americas and Oceania; esoteric, new and secular), each with its count and a sentence, a **Show all 793** button, a name search, and three rows of chips to begin from: where it began (the six continents), when it began (nine bands of years, by the earliest year the Atlas gives) and today. **Show all** opens the whole list, a hundred rows at a time, with the four ways of belief (and the deep past) as buttons, and selects for **Family** (the nine groups, with their families under them), **Where it began** (the continent of the country where the Atlas puts the beginning), **When it began**, **Still practised?** (*practised today* is living or revived in modern times; *no longer practised* is ended or became another tradition) and **Kind**; a **Something to read here** switch (a text of the tradition’s own that is in the Library, an old source it draws on, or a book about it, held in this copy; a text on a parent’s shelf does not count, so Christianity’s Bible does not make every denomination “have a text”); an **order** (A to Z, oldest first, most texts, and best match when a name is typed); a count (“109 of 793 traditions”) and **Clear filters**. Every choice shows how many it would give with the other filters kept, and a choice that would give none is dimmed, so no click ends on an empty page; a search that finds nothing says how many it would find without the other choices (`trlCounts`; `tests/tradlist.test.js` checks that every count equals what the choice gives, over thousands of choices from 120 starting points). The choices live in the address (`?b=one&r=Asia&s=now&x=1`; the older names q, b, g, f, s, k and n still work, and the older status codes L, X and H), and a choice changes the page in place without a reload: each is a step in the browser’s history (`history.pushState`), so Back and Forward step through them and a copied link shows the same list, while typing in the box only replaces the address, so it leaves no trail and is never interrupted. The last choice (never the search words) is kept on this device (`tradlist.last`) and shown, with “Start again”, when the page is opened with no address, so the “← All traditions” link of a tradition’s page returns to the list you were in. A row shows the name and when it began, the belief it is found under (and “also …”), the family, the kind, whether it is practised, and how many texts, as the tradition’s own page counts them. A belief label is a way to find a tradition, not a verdict, and the page says that dates and places are approximate. Browse by belief is unchanged. On a phone the belief buttons scroll sideways and the selects sit two to a row.

### The page of one tradition, and the family tree

A tradition's page (`#/atlas/tradition/<id>`, under Traditions → All traditions) is the place to study one religion as a whole. Under its title it says which belief it is often described as, then offers jump buttons and **Study it as a whole**: the texts of the Library that are its own (found through the tradition it grew from, `shelvesOfTradition`), its stations in the Journey (a chip called **Journey →**; the Journey's view of the religion links back with “The full page for …”, or, for the ten views that name a family of religions, with the list of that family), the map, its roots and branches, its calendar and holy days, how it is practised, and worship together. The sections below are Dated records (the page used to call this one Timeline), Presence over time, Relationships, Practices, Texts in the catalogue (the catalogue's records; the Library's books about it have their own heading), Sites, Calendar and one Related strip; a tradition with no branches, sites or relationships says so once, in one line at the foot (“Not recorded for this tradition: …”). **Roots and branches** shows where it came from (the parents, root first), itself, and what grew from it (each branch with how many are below it); **Presence over time** begins with **the route it took**, the places where it first appears, in order. **Traditions → Family tree** (`#/traditions/tree`) is every tradition that has a recorded parent or children, as nested lists that open one at a time, with a name filter and a belief filter, and `?open=<id>` opens the way to one tradition and marks it. The Atlas records one parent per tradition where scholars agree on one (Islam is a root of its own); the tree says so, and each page lists the other relationships.

**The family tree as a branch timeline (built and wired, 18 November 2026).** `src/js/6zzb-tree.js` (the page: `treeBranchView(body, query)`) and `src/js/2z-tree-core.js` (the logic) draw each of the 561 traditions that sit in a tree as a line on the same year axis as the history timeline (no year 0, the same zoom), from where it forks off its parent’s line to where the Atlas says it ended: an arrowhead for one still going, a stop bar for one that ended, a hand-off mark for one that gave way to a successor, a pale stretch where a beginning or an end is uncertain, a mark at the left edge for a line that began before 3500 BCE. It opens on about 1400 BCE to 2000 CE and **collapsed to the 61 families that have branches** (the 232 that stand alone are listed under the chart); the plus sign opens one level, “Open all N below” in an entry opens the rest, `open=<id>` opens the way to one tradition and marks it, the keyboard follows the usual tree pattern (arrows; right opens, left closes), the filters are name, belief, family and still going or ended, and the same tree reads as a nested **List**. Children sit below their parent with the **latest fork nearest**, so a link never crosses a line (a test checks all 500 links). An entry has its dates, its place in the tree, its belief label, what it grew from and what grew from it, and **the other relationships the tree leaves out** (influence, conflict, absorption…), drawn as dotted links for the chosen line only, and the ways on (its page, the map, what else was happening then in the history timeline). Three records (Saint Thomas Christians, Newar Buddhism, Celtic Christianity) have an earliest year before their parent’s: the Atlas records a start as a window, the windows overlap in all three, the line begins where they first do, and the entry says so in words that call it uncertainty, not a mistake. `Pages.traditions` in `6zz-traditions.js` calls `treeBranchView(body, query)` in place of `traditionsTree(body, query)` for the `tree` view (the older list stays as the fallback when `6zzb-tree.js` is not loaded). Test: `node tests/tree.test.js`.

**Compare traditions, step by step (built 18 November 2026, waiting for its place in the menu).** The page is `#/steps` (`src/js/6zzc-chain.js`, which registers `Pages.steps`; the logic is `src/js/2z-chain-core.js`; the styles are the `@chain` block of `app.css`). Choose two traditions (type a name and the Atlas’s own names are offered, or take one of seven ready-made pairs) and it shows **the fewest recorded connections that join them**, as a chain of cards on a thin rail. The graph is the Atlas’s own: its 500 recorded connections plus, for each of the 346 traditions whose parent no connection of the kinds that say so already names, the parent link (labelled “Recorded lineage”), taken in either direction. The search is exact (a test checks it against a plain search over 400 random pairs); chains of equal length are ranked by how many of their steps have a cause written down, then by name, so the same two traditions always give the same chain, and the others are a Previous and Next button away. A chain follows connections, not time, and says so when its steps are not in date order. Each **step** gives what happened in the Atlas’s own sentence (so the direction is the record’s), when and where, **what is said to have brought it about** (a cause from a fixed list of ten, each marked Documented, Reported or Disputed), a summary in the project’s own words, the sources with their grade (A a record from the time, B a scholarly work, C a reference summary) and, for a source the Library holds, the Library’s own way to read it; where there is no row it says, in the data’s own words, “How this came about is not recorded here.” A thin **strip** above the steps puts the chain on the history timeline’s years with a few of the larger events of the world in the same years (not the religion strand, which the steps are), each a way into `#/history`. Two choices narrow the search: **by cause** (“follow only what was carried by trade”) and **by kind of connection** (“what grew from what”, or “contact”); where one tradition is recorded as an ancestor of the other but the shortest chain goes through contact, the page says so and offers descent (Christianity to Methodism goes through conversions in Fiji unless it is asked what grew from what, and then runs down the line through Catholicism and Anglicanism). With nothing chosen the page lists, under “What each cause carried”, every row by cause, each a way into its pair. When there is no chain it says “No recorded chain”, which is not the same as no connection, with what is recorded for each. The address keeps the pair, which chain, the kind and the causes: `#/steps?a=islam&b=sikhism&r=1&k=contact&c=trade,court`.

* **The cause rows** (`tools/atlas_src/json/interaction_causes.json`, the vocabulary and notes in `interaction_causes_meta.json`, built into `atlas/causes.js`): 179 rows in the pilot, 164 for connections and 15 for parent links (`link_id` is a connection’s id, or `lineage:` and the child’s id for a parent link no connection already states). A row has a `summary` (60 to 560 characters, the project’s own words), one to three `causes` (each a `cause` from the fixed list travel, trade, conquest, translation, mission, schism, reform, court, shared_origin, independent; a `status` documented, reported or disputed; a `note`), an optional `route`, `sources` (`title`, `kind`, a `grade` A to C that its kind decides, an optional https `url` that is an English Wikipedia page, an optional `lib` that must be a Library text, an optional `note`), a `confidence` (“sure” or “less sure”: a row with a `check` is always less sure, and the `check` says what a second reader should look at) and, only if it has no source, a `source_note` (then every cause must be “reported”). There is no “unknown” cause: a connection with no row has none recorded, and the page says so. The build **stops** on a row for a connection that does not exist or whose parent link a connection already states, a repeated or out-of-order row, a cause outside the list, a status that does not fit, a source without a grade or an https address, a Library text that is not there, or a documented cause with no source (`check_causes` in `tools/build_atlas.py`; `tests/causes.test.js` runs it on 25 kinds of broken row). Rules for writing a row: hand research only; name a work (author, title, year) only where it is known to exist, otherwise leave the source out and mark the row reported; never write a cause that cannot be supported; neutral words (a test refuses the loaded ones); a disputed cause says in its own note that the matter is argued.
* **Review state: unchecked.** Every row was written by hand from the standard accounts and each source is named. A second reader (another session, not a specialist) has read all 179 rows against pages that were opened, in three passes (34 on 18 November 2026, 54 on 21 November and the last 91 on 22 November; `second_reading` in `interaction_causes_meta.json` lists the ids and the passes, and `review_note` says how each was read: the 21 November pass partly at the opening section of a Wikipedia page only, the 22 November pass on whole pages, 16 rows also in the Library’s own texts); every Wikipedia address was looked up on 18 November; 20 rows are marked “less sure” with a note of what to look at, and none has been read by a specialist (`FACT_CHECK.md`, “Second reading, part 2, 2b and 4”). The page says at the top that every row has been read a second time by another reader and none by a specialist (`review` in `interaction_causes_meta.json` is what shows that note). Wikidata does not hold causes.
* **To add a row**, append it to `interaction_causes.json` in order of `link_id`, run `python3 -I tools/build_atlas.py` (it stops on a bad row), then `node tests/causes.test.js` and `node tests/chain.test.js`. **To wire the page**, add the key `steps` to the sections table (`2z-sections-core.js`) under Traditions → Compare; until then it opens at `#/steps` without the tab strip. Tests: `node tests/causes.test.js`, `node tests/chain.test.js`.

### The neutral voice (18 November 2026: UX-22, UX-23 and the Movements lede of `UX_AUDIT.md`)

The maker: “stay as neutral as possible. i’m not building this to direct opinions. its only a tool for people to form their own opinions.” The same four rules hold for every tradition, mainstream ones included, in the Atlas data (`tools/atlas_src/json/`: the one-line summaries and founders of `traditions.json`, the notes of `texts.json`, `sites.json`, `practices.json`, `interactions.json`, `presence.json`) and in every page text: **(1)** a revelation, vision, séance, dictation or miracle is a claim of the tradition that reports it and is written so (“held to have been revealed”, “said to”, “reported”, “in the Muslim account”), never as an event in the tool’s voice; **(2)** a title is not a status (“the Prophet”, “the Mahdi”, “(the Buddha)” become the name, with “known as” where the title matters); **(3)** what a tradition holds is attributed (“Belief in…”, “Traditions that hold…”, “holding that…”), and a verb of worship (“worship of”, “devotion to”, “faith in”) and the names of what is worshipped stay as they are; **(4)** secular facts (dates, places, founders as historical persons, what a text says) are not weakened. For living groups, current movements and recent conflicts each statement is labelled documented, reported or claimed, or disputed. A teaser or a lead (the “Look closer” cards, Common ground) carries its grade word or asks a question and shows the differences beside the likenesses; no superlative without a source. `tests/neutral.test.js` keeps this: it scans the Atlas prose, the code lines of `src/js`, the stories, Common ground and its passages, the family sentences and the belief labels, fails with the file and line (or the Atlas record and field), and has a short list of exceptions with their reasons. Every change of this round, old line and new line, is in `FACT_CHECK.md` (“The neutral voice”). When you write a new summary or note, write what the tradition says or does, name the one who says it, and run the test.

### Phone and accessibility (18 November 2026; UX-10, 11, 14, 17, 29, 30, 31 and 34 of `UX_AUDIT.md`)

**Touch targets (UX-11).** On a coarse pointer or under 760 px (`@media (pointer:coarse),(max-width:760px)`, one block at the end of `src/css/app.css`, kept by `tests/tap.test.js`) every control has a touch area of at least 40 px on its short side, and nothing changes on a desktop with a mouse. In the flow it is a minimum size or padding: chips (including the Master timeline's, which a rule of two classes had left 23 px high), segmented buttons, small buttons, inputs, sliders, switches and tick-box rows, accordion summaries, More, links that stand alone in a list, a table cell or a timeline entry, the heat-map cells, the reader's bar icons (34 to 40 px). Where a control is drawn on a chart or in a margin the drawing stays and an invisible `::after` carries the touch area (a bordered control needs one more pixel of inset, because an absolute box starts inside the border): the family tree's `+179` toggles and names (a 40 px area around each, which in a dense stretch is shared with the rows above and below, so a tap goes to the nearest row: 28 px, the row pitch, by 41 px; a phone would need the list view or a taller row for more), the "i" buttons (40 by 40), a paragraph's bookmark (40 high, 30 wide: the other 10 px would lie over the text), the marks of the History line (35 by 21, as much as a 30 px lane allows); in an SVG a transparent stroke that does not scale (`vector-effect:non-scaling-stroke`) is the touch area (the points of the map of centres: a 5 px point reached from 20 px away). ### Atlas: religion on a flat map, through time

A time slider from 340,000 BCE to 2026 over a flat world map, where traditions appear, spread, branch, meet, clash, blend and fade. It is built on the *World Religion Atlas* database (the package held 721 traditions, 2,199 presence rows and 382 relationships; 72 traditions, 72 presence rows and 118 relationships were added on 17 November 2026, so the Atlas now has 793 traditions, 2,307 text records, 488 sites, 2,271 presence rows, 500 relationships and 72 practices) and follows that package’s handoff rules exactly: signed years with no year 0, the presence rule, the piecewise slider scale and era bands, and visible uncertainty everywhere.

* **The map.** Country-level rows are large soft blobs and place rows are sharper points, sized by prominence (an approximate share of the local population, never a headcount) and coloured **and shaped** by family group, so colour is never the only cue. Rows fade in across their uncertain start and fade out after they end, so growth and decline read as change; an origin pulses when a tradition begins. Arcs show the relationships active at that year (lineage, exchange, pressure). Drag, scroll or pinch to zoom; labels fade in for the most prominent traditions. Zoom and pan glide on a spring, and a drag let go keeps coasting. The coastlines are drawn once and then moved and scaled while you pan and zoom, and redrawn sharp a moment after you stop, so the map stays smooth at the whole-world view where it has the most to draw. `tools/atlas_frames.js` is a paste-in console script that measures the main-thread time of each frame for a set of zooms, drags and glides.
* **Time.** One slider with the era bands above it and a density strip (how many traditions are active in each family group across the whole track). Arrow keys, Home/End and PageUp/Down step it; Play runs a time-lapse at ¼× to 4×; **Story** stops at the 259 biggest moments in turn. The slider never lands on year 0.
* **Click a mark to dive in.** Clicking a tradition on the map opens a panel beside it (docked on a wide screen, a slim bar that opens upward on a narrow one): when it began, its **own timeline** of dated records (its beginning, the places and rock art, texts, contacts, branches and endings, in order), with **Earlier / Later** to step through its history while the map moves to each place and time, and a marker for where the slider is now; **beliefs and practice** (its Religion-section page, stations, words to know, the practices it used, and a search of every text for its name); **texts** (each opens in the reader where the Library holds it, on the list and on the timeline alike, and where it does not the row says so plainly: listed in the Library without a copy to read, an oral tradition, lost, not yet deciphered, or not in the Library; the Library’s own books about the tradition and the old sources of a revival are listed there too, as on its page); and its **family** (parents, branches, relatives, which you can follow in place). Long timelines show the most important and can show all. Indigenous and oral traditions carry a short note that their dates are rough and that some of the knowledge is restricted by the communities themselves; the Library holds what the open record contains (mostly outsiders’ accounts, labelled as such) and the map shows only the places that record names. Every tradition’s full page has the same timeline, with an “on the map” link for each record.
* **Selecting and filtering.** The click also highlights the tradition’s presence, ancestors, descendants and relationships. Filter by family group, family, **practice** ("who used meditation, drumming, sacred plants") or search by name, original name or founder. Year, selection, filters, lens, zoom and pan live in the address, so any view can be shared.
* **A tradition page for every one of the 793** (every field has an empty state): dates with their confidence, founder, origin, family tree, presence over time, relationships written as sentences, practices, texts, sites, and how followers are counted (only six traditions have a total, from Pew Research Center 2020; elsewhere it says “no reliable count”). Prehistoric horizons say they are *inferred from archaeology*; Indigenous and oral traditions say their dates mark first attestation.
* **Text catalogue.** All 2,307 records, filterable by tradition, family group, era, region, kind of text and status, with **Show on map**. Records that match an entry in the Lamplighter Project’s own Library (568 do, 525 of them with a readable copy; 24 were matched by hand on 18 November 2026) open the reader where a public-domain translation is held, and every other record says plainly why it does not (`atlTextAccess` and `atlTextNone` in `src/js/2t-atlas-core.js`: read, part, parts, listed, none). These are catalogue records, not the texts themselves.
* **Bridges.** Meditate asks *Who else practised this?* and opens the Atlas filtered to that practice; the Gateway links chant, hymn and drumming to the Atlas and says plainly that binaural beats are a modern technique, never an ancient one; every Religion tradition page opens in the Atlas.
* **Reads for everyone.** A text list of what is on the map at the current year is kept for screen readers, the slider and map work from the keyboard, and the About tab lists every source, the Pew and Natural Earth credits, the conventions and the data’s limits.
* **Calendars.** What the Atlas year is called in about twenty traditions’ counts, shown under the slider and as a converter for any year: Hebrew, Islamic, Chinese, Buddhist, Hindu (Vikram, Shaka, Kali Yuga), Jain, Sikh, Coptic, Ethiopian, Solar Hijri, Zoroastrian, Bahá’í, Roman, Olympiads, Seleucid, Japanese era names, Tibetan, the **Maya Long Count with Tzolk’in and Haab’**, and Mexica year bearers. Year numbers are shown as a range where the other calendar’s new year falls inside the year; before a calendar’s epoch it says so, and before it was introduced the number is marked **counted back**. A chart shows when each was in use, a table shows the Islamic new year drifting eleven days a year while the solar ones stay put, and each calendar says how it works and what it is for. Maya, Chinese, Aztec and Olympiad arithmetic is checked against fixed points (13.0.0.0.0 on 21 December 2012, 1984 as Jiazi, 1521 as 3 House, Olympiad 293 in 393 CE). This is the **Any year** chip of Timeline → Calendars; the day converter and the pages on the Sun, Moon, zodiac and astrology are described under “Calendars and the sky” below.
* **Maps of the world, as religions drew them.** Twelve schematic cosmologies (Babylon, Egypt, the Hebrew Bible, Greece, the medieval T-O map, Islam’s seven heavens, Mount Meru, China, the Maya, the Inca, the Norse, the Māori), each with its date, source and where the evidence is thin; a map of **eleven places peoples called the centre of the world**; a tool for **the direction each tradition turns to pray** from 27 cities (the Kaaba, Jerusalem, Bahjí and east, as great-circle bearings checked against published qibla bearings); and a table of which way was “up”. The map also has **viewing lenses**: Jerusalem at the centre with east up (medieval Christian), Mecca at the centre with south up (many medieval Muslim geographers), China at the centre with south up (early Chinese maps), Pacific- and India-centred. These are conventions, not copies of anyone’s map. Aboriginal songlines are deliberately not drawn: they are knowledge held in song by custodial communities, parts of which are restricted.

The data lives in an `atlas/` folder next to `Lamplighter.html` (like `texts/`), loaded on demand: `core.js` (about 1 MB) when the Atlas opens, the rest as needed. It is built from `tools/atlas_src/` by `python3 -I tools/build_atlas.py`; the coastlines are Natural Earth (public domain), simplified. `core.js` also carries `about`: for each tradition, the Library books written about it (a field record, a study, a history), turned round from `tools/library_about.json`, which says which tradition(s) each book describes; the tradition page lists them under Texts. `lore` is the same for revived religions: the old source texts they draw on (the Eddas for Heathenry), from `tools/library_lore.json`, listed above the books about it.

### World history, with religion woven in (a pilot, 18 November 2026)

The maker’s top priority for the Journey: “visualize the timeline and understand the big picture of what was going on with mankind and what time it was and how that all played out and interacted, so people get a real picture of human history”. The page is **`#/history`** (`src/js/6pw-history.js`, the logic in `2z-history-core.js`, the styles at the end of `app.css`, the data in `tools/atlas_src/json/world_*.json` built into `atlas/world.js`). It registers itself with the router; `history` is the first tab of the Timeline and the app’s landing page (`SEC_HOME` in `2z-sections-core.js`, set by the Navigation session on 18 November 2026). If `atlas/world.js` is missing (a data folder built before the world’s events were added) the page still opens, with the religion row alone from the Atlas’s own events and a sentence that says what is missing (`HIST_NO_WORLD` in `2z-history-core.js`; tested).

* **What it shows.** One horizontal line from 3500 BCE to 1900 CE on a linear scale (so a span is as long as it was; there is no year 0, so 1 BCE and 1 CE are one year apart), and a quiet row for each strand: **Religion** (the Atlas’s own beginnings of traditions and moments a ruler or state took up a faith, 142 of them, so the map, the tradition pages and this line tell one story), **Empires and states**, **Trade and migration**, **Science and technology**, **Ideas and arts**, **Disease and disaster**, and **Conflict** (a pilot of the later war section). A mark opens its entry beside the line (below it on a narrow screen). The strands can be put away, the line zoomed (buttons, plus and minus, or Ctrl and the wheel; “Whole span”; “Go to” an era) and scrolled, and the same events read as a plain dated **List**. The address keeps the zoom, the centre, the chosen event, the strands put away and the way of looking, so any view can be shared. A crowded row shows its most important marks first and says how many more appear as you zoom in; a row is never more than six lines high.
* **An entry** has: its strand and **how sure it is** (Documented: a record anyone can check fixes it; Reported: said by someone and not fixed by a record; Disputed: sources or scholars disagree, and the entry says where), its date with its confidence (the Atlas’s H, M, L, C), its places, a summary of one to three plain sentences in the project’s own words, the **texts of the Library to read beside it** (the same Read link, or the same plain sentence, as the Atlas panel), the traditions it touches (their pages, and the map at that year), **connected** events (part of, led to, carried, shaped, happened during, compare with, ended: a link that is a matter of debate carries a note saying so), **what else was happening** (a few from each other strand, the nearest and most important), and its **sources with a grade** (A a record from the time, B a scholarly work or database, C a reference summary) with the plain statement that the sources are named but not yet re-read.
* **A conflict** also has its **parties**, its **causes** (religious, political, economic or mixed, each with how well it is documented, none a verdict: “wars of religion” is a label scholars dispute) and **the dead as a range** with its unit, status and basis, or the plain words that no range is given.
* **A religion's events** (`#/history?t=<tradition id>`, 22 November 2026): one quiet chip, “Events that name X or a branch of it ✕”, keeps on the line, the list, the strand counts and “In view” only the events that name that religion or one that grew from it (the same rule as the Conflicts page); the ✕ shows every event again. A religion's page opens it from “In its time, N more”; the page's own N counts only the events that name the religion itself, so it is smaller than the filter's count (Christianity: 6 and 101). A conflict or settlement dated after 1900, which the line cannot draw, opens its entry on the Conflicts page, and a line under the strand chips says that the line stops at 1900.
* **The data model** (`world_events.json`, one object per event): `id`, `strand`, `label`, `year_start`, `year_end` (a span; none for a point), `circa`, `date_label` (the Atlas’s style, checked against the years by a test), `date_confidence`, `status` (documented, reported or disputed), `places` (name, lat, lon, optional `country_iso2`, `approx` for a region; Jerusalem is given with no country, as the Atlas does), `summary`, `sources` (`title`, `kind`, `grade`, optional `url` over https and `note`), `traditions` (Atlas ids), `texts` (Library ids), `importance` (1 to 5: how large it is on the whole-span line), `related` (`id`, `kind`, optional `note`) and, for a conflict only, `conflict` with `parties`, `causes` and `casualties` (`low`, `high`, `unit`, `status`, `note`) or `casualties: null` with a `casualty_note`. `world_strands.json` names the strands and `world_meta.json` the vocabularies (statuses, grades, confidences, links, causes) and the notes the page prints. Religion is **not** repeated in the file: `histReligion` (in the core) takes it from `atlas/events.js` by a rule written in the code and checked by a test (a beginning of importance 4 or 5, shown as a point with its window of uncertainty and dated as a window; a moment a ruler or state took up a tradition, importance 4 or 5; a major ending).
* **The pilot** was 86 events (the timeline has grown since: see “The expansion” below): 26 empires and states (Egypt’s Old Kingdom to the Meiji Restoration, with the Aztec and Inca states, Aksum and Mali), 11 on trade and migration, 14 on science and technology (writing to Darwin), 15 on ideas and arts, 12 on disease and disaster, and 8 conflicts (Kadesh, the Greco-Persian and Peloponnesian wars, the Punic Wars, the First Crusade, the Spanish campaigns in the Americas, the Thirty Years’ War, the Taiping Rebellion). 25 open a text of the Library, 74 name a tradition, 19 links join 18 of the events; 74 are documented, 3 reported and 9 disputed. It is **a first draft chosen by the author, not a ranking**; some regions and periods are thin. The words are held to the project’s neutrality rule by a test (no word of accusation or praise; every disputed entry says where sources differ; every reported one says what it rests on).
* **Review state: unchecked.** Every date, place and sentence was written by hand from the standard reference accounts, and every source is named, but none has yet been re-read against the entry and no date has been compared with Wikidata (CC0), which is the intended source for that check; it needs the maker’s go-ahead before anything is fetched (about 90 entities, one request every six seconds). The page says so in every entry. The sources are English Wikipedia pages (CC BY-SA, cited and not copied), the Slave Voyages database, and records from the time named by title.
* **The expansion** (BACKLOG D6, 18 November 2026): 207 events were added to the pilot, 293 in all, each written from pages the writer opened and read (English Wikipedia as plain text; extracts of records from the time at Boston University’s African Studies Center, LacusCurtius, New Advent and the National Archives; one open-access abstract). Britannica, UNESCO’s lists and several journals refuse automated reading and are not used. The new events have had a first reading only (the 86 of the pilot had a second): `world_meta.json` says so in the note the page prints, and `FACT_CHECK.md`, section “World history timeline expansion”, lists what each batch rests on and what was not opened. By region (the first place of each event, by hand): Europe and the Mediterranean 57, East Asia 40, Near East and Iran 36, Americas 33, Sub-Saharan Africa and Nubia 28, South Asia 24, North Africa and Egypt 23, Southeast Asia 14, Oceania and Australia 13, Central Asia 11, global 14; by strand: states 99, ideas 62, science 57, trade 37, disaster 30, conflict 8 (the conflicts file adds its own). The Line keeps at most five lanes a row at the whole span and more as it is zoomed (`histCap` in `6pw-history.js`), because empires that overlap in time cannot be separated by zooming. A source may link only to the hosts listed in `HOSTS` in `tests/history.test.js`; a new host is added there on purpose.
* **To add or correct an event**, edit `tools/atlas_src/json/world_events.json` (keep it in date order; `json.dumps(indent=1, ensure_ascii=False)` is its format) and run `python3 -I tools/build_atlas.py`: the build **stops** on a repeated id, an unknown strand, years the wrong way round, a missing source, a grade that does not fit its kind, an address that is not https, a tradition or Library text that does not exist, a conflict without its parties or causes, or a link to nothing. Then `node tests/history.test.js`.

### Calendars and the sky (BACKLOG D7, built 18 November 2026)

The maker’s idea (walk-through of 18 November 2026): how the Chinese calendar differs from the American one, the Greco-Roman calendar, how religion and the stars are tied together, and, for astrology, whether the right method is used “since the stars move”; and “i’m not building this to direct opinions. its only a tool for people to form their own opinions.” It is the **Calendars** tab of the Timeline (labelled just “Calendars” so that four tabs fit a phone; its pages are titled Calendars and the sky; the `/` search finds it by calendar, zodiac, horoscope, astrology, sidereal, precession and “calendars and the sky”). Seven chips: **Any day** (`#/sky`), **Any year** (`#/atlas/calendars`, the older year converter and its chart, unchanged), **Sun and moon** (`#/sky/moon`), **The zodiac** (`#/sky/zodiac`), **Where was the sun?** (`#/sky/sun`), **Astrology** (`#/sky/astrology`) and the **Year wheel** (`#/path/year`). Files: `src/js/2z-sky-core.js` (all the arithmetic and astronomy, pure, no network), `src/js/2z-data-astrology.js` (the astrology data), `src/js/6zub-sky.js` (the pages), the `@sky` block of `app.css`, `tests/sky.test.js`. **Nothing a person picks is saved, sent or put in the address** (the address is only read: `?d=2024-03-20&s=julian` opens the day pages on a day; a test greps the source for any use of the store, history, location or network).

* **Any day.** Day, month, year, era and whether the date is Gregorian or Julian in; fifteen answers out, each with the weekday and Julian Day above, a sentence on what that calendar counts (Sun, Moon or both; its starting point; how it closes the gap between the Sun’s year and the Moon’s months; when its day begins) and a label for **how it was reached**: *Exact arithmetic* (Gregorian, Julian, Hebrew, Coptic, Ethiopian, the Shaka national calendar, Nanakshahi, the Maya Long Count with Tzolk’in and Haab’ under the usual correlation, the Roman Kalends, Nones and Ides); *Astronomical computation, good to the day for about 1900 to 2100* (the Chinese calendar from the new Moon and the solar terms at Beijing time, the Persian calendar from the equinox before noon at Tehran, the Bahá’í calendar from the equinox and the sunset at Tehran from 2015, the Vikram year from the Hindu new-year new Moons); or *Varies by place and school*, which always gives a **range and never one confident date** (the Islamic date is the tabular one with the day before and the day after; the Vikram year gives both customs, the Chaitra and the Kartika year; the Julian, Hebrew, Roman, Thai and Chinese and Persian answers outside their sure years give “a day either side”, “up to three days”, or “one year”). A calendar that did not yet exist is marked *counted back*; before a calendar’s start it says so; the Chinese calendar is not computed before 1645 or after 2200. A day here is the civil day: the Hebrew, Islamic and Bahá’í days begin at sunset, which the row says. The rules are those of Reingold and Dershowitz, *Calendrical Calculations*, and the Sun, equinoxes and new Moon those of Meeus, *Astronomical Algorithms* (chapters 25, 27, 49), written afresh; delta-T is the Espenak and Meeus polynomial set. Not covered (the year converter still gives the year): Zoroastrian, Jain, Seleucid, the Japanese eras, Tibetan, the Aztec day count (its correlation is disputed) and the Hindu tithi and lunar month (they vary by region and school: only the new-year windows and the Vikram year are given).
* **How it was checked** (all against sources opened for this work, read only, nothing saved in the project): Meeus’s worked Julian-Day examples and his new-Moon example (to five decimals); the Hong Kong Observatory’s conversion tables (`hko.gov.hk`, 1901, 1928, 1950, 1984, 2012, 2033 and 2057: all 82 lunar-month starts and the four leap months identical, including 1928 when Beijing still kept local mean time); Chinese New Year 2024 to 2040 equal to the table the Practice calendar already uses; all 60 equinoxes and solstices of 2016 to 2030 within two minutes of the English Wikipedia table; Nowruz 2016 to 2031 and Naw-Rúz 2024 to 2031 against Wikipedia (the Naw-Rúz of 2026 is decided by less than a minute at Tehran’s sunset, so the row is flagged and the test only asks for “within a day”); Rosh Hashanah and the festivals of the existing calendar tests; the Lahiri and Fagan–Bradley values of 8 April 2024 (24.1961° and 25.0793°) from a third-party Swiss-Ephemeris listing; the Sun’s entry into Ophiuchus (29 or 30 November to 17 or 18 December) as EarthSky prints it for 2018 to 2021. The tabular Islamic date is only asked to be within a day of the sighted dates, as in the older tests.
* **Sun and moon.** The two lengths (365.24 and 29.53 days; twelve months 10.9 days short, thirteen 18.7 over), three ways to live with them (keep the Sun: Egyptian, Julian, Gregorian, Persian; keep the Moon: the Islamic year, round the seasons in about 34 years; keep both and add a month: the Hebrew seven in nineteen, the Babylonian cycle, Meton’s reported 432 BCE, the Chinese and Hindu rules), Greece and Rome (the Julian reform, Gregory’s ten days and who adopted when), a computed table of how far each count wanders from the seasons, and three small drawings (the year against the months, the Islamic new year going round the year in 35 steps, the nineteen-year cycle).
* **The zodiac.** A ring of three zodiacs with a **year slider from 3000 BCE to 3000 CE**: the thirteen IAU constellations and the Lahiri sidereal signs fixed to the stars, the twelve tropical signs turning with precession, the March equinox marked, and a live line saying which constellation the equinox is in and which tropical Aries lies over; five ready-made years (Babylon about 400 BCE, Hipparchus, Ptolemy, the zero of the Lahiri zodiac about 286 CE, today); a table of the three ways with the same fields each; precession (1° in about 72 years, a full turn in about 25,800); who found it and on whose word (Hipparchus, reported by Ptolemy’s *Almagest*; Newton for the cause; earlier-discovery claims named and called unsupported or controversial by the reference works). The **thirteen boundaries of the constellations along the ecliptic** (Scorpius 6.6° wide, Ophiuchus 18.6°, Virgo 44.0°) were derived for this project from Roman’s 1987 table of Delporte’s boundaries (Strasbourg catalogue VI/42, the copy distributed with astropy, B1875.0, read only; 13 numbers kept), by carrying points of the J2000 ecliptic back to 1875.0 and looking each up; they are good to about 0.01°. Precession is the IAU 2006 polynomial; the ayanamsas are their J2000 values (Lahiri 23.8571°, Fagan–Bradley 24.7405°) plus the precession.
* **Where was the sun?** A day (and an hour of Universal Time if wanted) in; out the Sun’s tropical sign, its sidereal sign by the Lahiri and by the Fagan–Bradley zero (with the Sanskrit rashi) and its IAU constellation, a strip drawing of the three rows with the Sun on it, and **the moment the Sun changes sign when it does so during the day**, in Universal Time, with the plain statement that the time of day and the time zone decide (29 November 2024: Scorpius into Ophiuchus at about 17:18 UT). The longitude is Meeus chapter 25 (about 0.01°, a quarter of an hour). No horoscope, no interpretation.
* **Astrology** (`#/sky/astrology?s=western|hindu|chinese|hellenistic|maya|aztec`). Six systems, each in the same four parts in the same order: (1) what it says, as its practitioners state it; (2) how it is worked out from a date, and what depends on the zodiac chosen; (3) what has been tested and published, with authors, year, venue, what was done and what was reported; (4) what practitioners and critics each say about those studies; then the sources read. **Every statement is labelled** Documented, Reported or claimed, or Disputed, and **every source graded** A (a record from the time: Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos*, Augustine’s *Confessions*), B (a scholarly work: Ertel 2009, Dean and Kelly 2003, Nienhuys 1997, Narlikar’s account of the Pune test, Johnson and Nye 2009) or C (a reference summary: English Wikipedia pages, Hindupedia), as the History timeline does; the note on each source says what was read. The studies listed: Carlson’s double-blind test (*Nature* 1985, as Ertel tabulates it) and Ertel’s reanalysis (*Journal of Scientific Exploration* 2009), the Mars effect tests from Gauquelin to the French committee (Nienhuys, *Skeptical Inquirer* 1997), Dean and Kelly’s time-twins test (2003), the Pune test of Indian astrologers (2008–09), Johnson and Nye on Dragon-year children (2009), births in Dragon years and the Japanese Fire Horse year, Augustine’s twins case; where a system has nothing published that was opened (the Maya and Aztec 260-day counts; the Four Pillars) the entry says **Not found** and says nothing else. No verdict is in the tool’s own voice (a test refuses “pseudo-”, “debunked”, “proven”, “superstition” and the like, every quotation is at most 30 words, and a verdict quoted from a source is never labelled documented). **Left out because it could not be opened** (reported to the project manager): Carlson’s paper itself (read through Ertel), Voas’s census study of 20 million marriages (the university page is gone), McRitchie’s 2016 reply to Dean and Kelly (blocked), Phillips, Ruth and Wagner’s *Lancet* 1993 study of Chinese birth years (blocked), Goodkind 1991 (read through Johnson and Nye), the Indian Supreme Court’s ruling on university courses in astrology (blocked), Tedlock’s *Time and the Highland Maya* and Boone’s book (descriptions only).
* **To change or add.** A calendar row is a builder in `skyRows` (`2z-sky-core.js`) with its sentence in `SKY_INFO`; the order is `SKY_ORDER`. A system or a study is an entry of `SKY_SYSTEMS` in `2z-data-astrology.js` with its sources in `SKY_REFS` (open the source first, grade it, say what was read); `node tests/sky.test.js` refuses a statement without a status or a source, a part that is out of shape, a loaded word, a long quotation, and an unused or ungraded source. A new page of the tab is a key in the Timeline row of `2z-sections-core.js` plus its view in `Pages.sky`.

### Gather: worship and study together, with no server

Everything lives in your browser. There are no accounts and nothing is sent anywhere unless you copy it out yourself. A gathering is a small **pack** (plain text, a file or a link) that you send by any channel you like; whoever receives it opens it in their own copy of the Lamplighter Project.

| Section | What it holds |
|---|---|
| **Home** | Start a silent sit or a morning rhythm at once; what is coming up on the calendar; your circles and services. |
| **Services** | An order of worship made of timed parts (welcome, silence, breath, reading, words, chant, reflection, practice, closing). Six forms to start from, each naming the tradition whose shape it borrows: a meeting in silence (unprogrammed Quaker form), a psalm and stillness (monastic office), sitting and kindness (Buddhist-style), readings from three traditions (interfaith), a short morning rhythm, an evening reflection. Readings are filled from the Library in English. Edit anything, run it full-screen with bells (or read it aloud), print it. A **shared start time** makes every device follow the same clock, so people in different places are at the same step at the same moment and latecomers join where everyone is. |
| **Circles** | A study circle walks the Religion path one station a week, kept in step by the calendar rather than anyone's progress. Each week brings a meeting guide (welcome, silence, the story, a reading, conversation questions, a practice, closing) you can run, save, share or print. Members exchange short **reflections** as signed notes. |
| **Calendar** | The next fifteen months of holy days, computed in the browser: Western and Orthodox Easter, Ash Wednesday to Pentecost, Advent, the Jewish calendar (Rosh Hashanah to Shavuot), the Islamic calendar (calculated, so within a day or two of the moon-sighted date), Lunar New Year to 2040, and fixed days from many traditions. Hold a gathering or add a day to your own calendar (.ics). |
| **Share and keys** | Open a pack you were sent (paste, link or file); make a signing key that lives only in this browser; back up or restore everything. |

How it stays trustworthy without a server: packs are compressed and URL-safe; a pack can be **signed** (ECDSA P-256 via the browser's WebCrypto) so people see a short fingerprint beside a name, a changed note stops verifying, and a forged one never does; everything that arrives is **sanitised** (unknown parts dropped, lengths and times clamped) and only ever shown as text. A link works only for people who can reach your copy of Lamplighter.html, so the pack text and the file are the universal way.

### Citing a passage, a whole text, a notebook and the app (20 November 2026; the second of the research-tool features)

The maker: “make this a really solid researching platform and tool.” **Cite** writes a citation in the forms scholars use: a **Chicago** note and bibliography entry, **BibTeX** (LaTeX, Zotero, JabRef) and **RIS** (Zotero, EndNote, Mendeley). One small dialog (`src/js/6zzg-cite.js`: the app’s `modal()`, a Chicago | BibTeX | RIS switch whose last choice is kept as `citeFormat` in the existing settings, a preview, **Copy** and **Download** as `lamplighter-cite-<key>.bib`, `.ris` or `.txt`) opens from the reader’s selection bar (under ⋯, last), a text’s source card (**Cite this text**), the Random card’s row of actions, a notebook page (**Cite the sources on this page…**) and a notebook’s menu (**Export bibliography…**: one entry per cited text, found from the links the clips keep back to their places), and, for the app itself, from Methods and standards and Setup. The rules are `src/js/2z-cite-core.js` (pure, `tests/cite.test.js`): one record per source built from what the Library holds, and three writers from it. **Nothing is invented.** The Library has no separate author, translator or year fields, only the free-text edition statement `by` (mostly “Name, Title (Place: Publisher, Year)”) and the title, so the core reads that shape where it is plain and leaves the rest whole in the record’s note (BibTeX `note`, RIS `N1`, and a line in the dialog): a name is turned round only when every word is a plain capitalised name, “and others” becomes “et al.”, a year is taken only when the bracket names one edition (the year of the edition hosted, never of composition), a scan is cited by the printed edition with a page only where the text has its printed page marks, a Bible by book, chapter and verse, a verse range by its name, and the section as the Library names it. “Accessed through The Lamplighter Project, release …” is a separate line with the day from the device’s clock (never stored). The only address is a text’s own source address (https); the app’s own (`APP.url`, empty until it has one) is left out while empty, and the app is cited by a corporate author, the release stamp and its year. Over all 1,448 texts the test checks that every name, title, place, publisher, edition and year in a record is in the Library’s own statement or title, and that the BibTeX and RIS of the whole Library parse. **The verse (21 November 2026, AA-11 of `ALPHA_AUDIT.md`):** a selection in a Bible text is cited by its verses, “Daniel 9:8” or “Daniel 9:7–8”, read from the verse numbers the text carries (`sup.vn`: the verse in effect at the start of the selection and at its last character, `rdVerseSpan` and `citeVerses` in `6k-reader.js`, `citeBibleRef(book, heading, v1, v2)` in `2z-cite-core.js`); when no number can be found, or the selection runs over two sections, or the heading is not a chapter, the citation names the chapter as before and nothing is guessed.

## Design notes

* **Pure sine waves** per ear (harmonics would add unwanted extra beats); phase-continuous sweeps; safety limiter with make-up gain cancelled so volume maps to documented dBFS.
* **Visuals never flash at the beat rate** (motion is slowed ≤ ~1.5 cycles/s; freezes with reduced-motion).
* Experiments: randomised blocks of two (one A, one B), analysed as paired differences; only the pre-declared primary outcome is a real test, the rest are Holm-corrected and labelled exploratory.
* Everything is stored in your browser (`localStorage`). Export a backup from Setup now and then.

While a text is open, the app's own sidebar, page header and tabs are hidden, so the first thing on the screen is the text (it used to start 781 px down a 1024×768 screen and about 1,370 px down a phone; it now starts about 75 px down). One slim bar stays at the top: back to the Library, the title with **Page N of M · P%** and a thin progress line, **Contents**, **Find**, a **bookmark** button, **Aa** (reading settings) and a **⋯** menu (read aloud, find, save, marks, keyboard shortcuts, full screen). It slides away as you read down and comes back when you scroll up or tap the text. Tapping the title (or Contents, or Find) opens a sheet, a side drawer on a computer and a bottom sheet on a phone. It is four quiet tabs (since 18 November 2026, UX-04 of `UX_AUDIT.md`): **Contents** (the list of sections and how far through you are; this is what opens, from the title, the Contents button and the **c** key), **Find** (find in this text, with its matches), **Highlights** (the highlights, notes and bookmarks of this text; tap one to jump to it) and **About** (the book’s card: title, author, rights, *Where this text comes from*, the scans, the section menu, Save, text size, how to read, random page, *I have read this* and the shelf). The repeated five places and the crumb are gone from it (the back arrow and the ⋯ menu go to the Library). Escape closes the sheet; the arrow keys move between the tabs.

### How to read: Scroll, Book pages or PDF sheets (18 November 2026)

The maker could not tell *By section* from *Scroll*, and they were right to ask: they are the same page, read the same way, and differ only in where the text stops. The chooser (the layers button in the top bar, **How to read** in the reader's sheet and in the **Aa** panel, and the **v** key) now offers **three styles, each with a small drawing, a plain name and one line**: **Scroll** (*Read straight down, as on a web page.*), **Book pages** (*Turn pages like a book or an e-reader: tap the sides or swipe.*) and **PDF sheets** (*Pages one above the other, like a PDF, with zoom.*). Under Scroll is one switch, **Stop at the end of each section**: on, it is the old *By section*; off, the old *Scroll*. Its line says what it does in the state it is in (*On: one section at a time, with Previous and Next buttons.* / *Off: the next section follows on as you read.*), so what the chooser says is what happens (UX-15: Scroll’s own line used to promise that the next section follows on while the switch, on by default, stopped it). The default stays as it was, one section at a time: changing it would change how every text opens for everyone who never chose, and the progress marks (*Mark read & next*) assume it. Nothing is lost and saved choices keep working: `reader.layout.mode` still holds `section`, `scroll`, `page` or `sheet` (`rlStyleOf` and `rlModeFor` in `2z-pager-core.js` turn them into a style and a switch and back, and every link with `?m=section`, `?w=1`, `?m=page` or `?m=sheet` opens as it did); the switch is also kept as `reader.stop`, so choosing Scroll again from Book pages comes back stopping at sections or not, as the reader had it (it begins on, as the old default was By section). The **v** key goes round the three styles and names the one it lands on. The old row of four buttons in the sheet is a collapsed *How to read: Scroll, one section at a time* line. Tests: `tests/pager.test.js`, `tests/reader-chrome.test.js`.

Every text opens in whichever way you last chose.

**Sheets.** The text is laid out as page-sized sheets, white (or the theme's own colour) on a grey desk, one above the other, and you scroll through them with the wheel, the trackpad, a finger or the scroll bar, as in a PDF viewer. Each sheet has a number at its foot (*3 / 11* is the third sheet of this section); the footer says *Page 57 of 110* (the reader's own page, a fixed 300 words, the same in every view) and *Sheet 3 of 11 · 3 min left in section*. A sheet is as wide as the screen up to 820 px less a gutter, in A4, US Letter or Book proportions, and has margins like paper. The bar over the sheets zooms from 70% to 220% (the type grows with the sheet, so every zoom shows the same words on each sheet), fits the sheet to the width, changes the paper, and goes to a page; the bar fades while you scroll and comes back when the pointer moves or the screen is touched. Space and Page Down move a sheet, Shift-Space and Page Up go back, Home and End go to the first and last sheet of the section, ] and [ to the next and previous section, **g** to Go to page, Ctrl-Home and Ctrl-End to the very start and end; the arrow keys scroll as they always do. It is the same text as in the other views (highlights, notes, bookmarks, find, read aloud and the word cards all work), and the place is kept when you change view. Only the sheets near the screen are filled with text and only three sections are drawn at a time, so the 15-million-character Mahabharata scrolls as lightly as a short text; the page count of a section is measured when it first comes up, so the scroll bar's length is an estimate that settles as you read (and a text of several million letters at 200% zoom can reach the height a browser allows a scroll to be, in which case the later sheets sit a little off).

**Page breaks, and bars that stay.** (17 November 2026, on the maker’s request: page dividers where the text scrolls, constant access to every feature, a top bar that can be shown or not; and that a reader must still be able to refer to original page numbers and sections.) **Page breaks.** In *By section* and *Scroll*, where the text runs on in one long column, a line with a small *Page 12* label marks where each page of the reader’s own count begins (a page is about 300 words, the same on every screen, and is the page in the bar and in *Go to page*). A section that begins a new page has its break above its title. Aa → *Page breaks* chooses *Numbered*, *Line* (a rule and no number) or *None*; the breaks are not part of the text, so selecting across one copies no “Page 12”, and no highlight, bookmark, note or search result moves. Pages and Sheets have their own page edges and do not draw them. **The reader’s page is not the printed page, and the card says so.** The builders dropped running heads and page numbers when the texts were made (the few “[p. 182]” in the text are the book’s own cross-references); for the texts whose printed page numbers have since been found (next paragraph) the reader shows the printed page, and for every other text it shows its own page and never presents it as the printed one. A tap on a break opens a card with the text and edition, the section (number of the total, and its name), the reader’s page, and a line that says where the printed pages are for this kind of text: for a **scan**, that the scan has every page with its printed number, with a button that opens the Internet Archive’s own scan searched at the words that follow the break (a text of several volumes asks which volume); for a **typed** text, that the source’s web edition keeps the printed page numbers, with a link to it; for a **Bible**, that it is cited by chapter and verse. *Copy reference* puts on the clipboard the text and edition, *Section 3 of 14, “Chapter 2”*, *Reader page 12 of 340 (about 300 words a page, the same on every screen; not the printed page)* and the source address, and nothing that is not known. While they are away a thin line at the foot keeps the section and page in view (the paged views have their own foot). Pages and Sheets sit inside the bars when these are kept, and the Sheets’ zoom, paper and *Go to…* buttons stay on the screen instead of fading. **Every feature is one tap away.** On a wide screen the bar has buttons for the view, highlights and notes, read aloud, contents, find, bookmark, reading settings, full screen and hide; on a phone it keeps contents, find, bookmark, settings and the menu. The **⋯** menu holds every feature whatever the width: the four views (the one in use ticked), contents, find, highlights/notes/bookmarks, bookmark this place, go to a page, the details and the original edition, reading settings, read aloud, full screen, the bars, save, keyboard shortcuts, a random page, the Library. **Printed page numbers (17 November 2026).** The maker’s rule is that a reader must be able to refer to the original page numbers and sections, which matters in religious study. The texts were made from OCR or typed copies with the printed page numbers dropped, so `tools/texts_pages.py` finds them again and keeps them beside each text as `texts/pg-<id>.js` (listed in `texts/pgs.js`, as the pictures are kept; 8 MB in all). **Scans (Internet Archive).** For 966 items it fetches the three small files the Archive keeps (`_page_numbers.json`: the printed number it read on each leaf; `_hocr_pageindex.json.gz` and `_hocr_searchtext.txt.gz`: the OCR page by page, the same OCR as our text), about 582 MB; for the 149 items that have only the heavier files, their `_djvu.xml` (the OCR page by page, 1,126 MB) and `_scandata.xml`; all cached in `tools/cache/pages/`. **Where the numbers come from, in order of trust.** (1) The Archive’s own list of printed numbers, when the number it gives each leaf is really in the first or last words of that leaf’s text. The Archive counts its page list and its text index from different places in different scans (leaf n is entry n in some and n−1 in others), so the tool tries seven offsets for each scan and takes the one where the numbers are really on their pages (the right one scores 0.7 to 0.9, a wrong one about 0). (2) If no offset stands out, an offset worked out from the Archive’s own search inside the scan (`calibrate`). (3) If that cannot settle it either, or the Archive read few numbers, or there is only a `_djvu.xml`, the numbers read from the pages’ own words: a number alone among the first or last four words of a page, kept only where the same step (number minus page) is found on five pages close together, and then given to the pages in between (marked as inferred). (4) Otherwise the text keeps no marks: a wrong page number is worse than none. **Typed texts (Project Gutenberg).** The page numbers come from the book’s own markup: the EPUBs kept for the pictures hold them for 61 texts, and the web editions held by Project Gutenberg’s own mirror (gutenberg.pglaf.org; 115 editions, 81 MB, one every six seconds, because gutenberg.org itself was down) for 26 more. The web editions of the other 101 carry no page numbers (the producers did not keep them), and 20 more have no web edition on the mirror. **Matching.** Every run of five words that occurs once in our text and once in the pages, kept in order, is an anchor; a page’s start is placed between its two nearest anchors and then moved onto the page’s first words after its running head. A mark is `[section, paragraph, [[offset, label], …]]`; a label is a number (arabic or roman) with a volume in front for several volumes (`2:214`) and a `~` when the number was not read from the page but follows from the numbers either side. A text is graded **a**, **b** or **c** by how much of it lies between close anchors (97%, 85%, 60%); below c nothing is kept. **Result:** 1,031 texts have their printed page numbers marked (944 scans and 87 typed; 448,442 marks; grade a 1,011, b 18, c 2). **In the reader** a page that begins at the start of a paragraph is a break labelled *p. 214* (instead of the reader’s own page break), one that begins inside a paragraph is a small marker at that place (it has no text of its own, so no highlight, bookmark or search result moves), a tap on either opens a card (edition, section, how the number was found, how far to trust it, the reader’s own page and that it is not the printed page, the scan at this passage, *Copy reference*); the bar says *p. 214 · reader page 12 of 340 · Section 3 of 14*; a copied quotation names the printed page it begins on; **Go to a printed page** (menu, or the **p** key) finds *214*, *p. 214*, *xii*, *2:214*; the Library marks each such text with a *printed page numbers* chip; saved copies keep the markers (*[p. 214]* in plain text, *\*[p. 214]\** in Markdown, a small marker in a web page and a real page break in an e-book). Commands: `python3 -I tools/texts_pages.py fetch` (the Archive’s page files, six at a time), `fetchxml` (the `_djvu.xml` of scans without them), `fetchpg2 [--pause=6] [--limit=N]` (the Gutenberg web editions of the 135 typed texts whose EPUB has no page numbers, about 200 MB, from Project Gutenberg’s own mirror gutenberg.pglaf.org because gutenberg.org answered 503 “retry in 3600 seconds” on 17 November: one request at a time, six seconds apart, no retries, and the first refusal stops the run and says the site’s Retry-After, so a limiter is never pushed; run it again later and it carries on), `fetchpg` (the same, six at a time: not for Gutenberg), `calibrate --only=<items>`, `build [--only=<ids>]` (match every text and write the files; run it again after the texts are rebuilt, because a changed text makes its file stale and the reader then ignores it), `verify --only=<ids> [--n=8]` (asks the Archive’s own search which page a phrase is on and compares). Tests: `node tests/pages.test.js`. After rounds 21 to 23 (17 November 2026) **1,073 texts carry printed page numbers**; six of the 56 new texts (`x-str`, `a-fft`, `a-wcb`, `a-lsc`, `k-aly`, `a-roq`) are from items on which the Archive keeps no page data, and `k-dun` (a sacred-texts keyboarding) is unconfirmed, so they have none.

**Time left** is an estimate from your own pace: the Lamplighter Project times how long you stay on a page, ignores flicks and long pauses, blends the rest into a running figure that starts at an easy 225 words a minute, and keeps it in your browser (`reader.cpm`). **Ask an AI** (on selected words, and on the Word help card). A plain button, *Ask an AI*, opens a small panel with the words you selected (or the sentence a word is in, and the word), their citation (the title, the section, the printed page where the text has them, the edition and where it is from), four questions (*What does this mean?*, *How is this connected to other traditions or texts?*, *Who wrote this, and when?*, *What do scholars disagree about here?*) and a box for your own. One button, *Copy the question and open Gemini*, copies a ready-made prompt to the clipboard and opens the AI's own site in a new tab (Gemini first; ChatGPT, Claude and Copilot are one choice away in the panel, kept in this browser as `ask.ai`). **The app sends nothing and holds no key**: the button is a plain link (`target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"`) that a click follows, the copying is on the same click, *Show exactly what will be copied* shows the text first, and the panel says in one line that the words you selected go wherever you paste them. If you are not sure of a source, say so. Do not invent references.* / *Keep to this passage and my question. If the translation or edition matters, say how. **Contents pages and indexes (18 November 2026).** The maker saw the contents and index pages of scanned books badly laid out, in *Ancient Records of Egypt*: entries ran on, the dots of a leader broke across lines, two page numbers sat on one line, nothing stood in a column. **The cause** is how the scans' text was made, not the reader: the OCR text has the lines of a page joined into paragraphs (right for prose), so a contents page of *Reign of Pepi I .......... 295-315 Hammamat Inscriptions .......... 295-301* became one wrapped paragraph. The text is not changed (every highlight, bookmark and printed-page marker is kept by paragraph and character offset), so the reader finds the entries (`src/js/2z-lines-core.js`) and draws each on a line of its own: the title, a dotted rule that fills the line (the book's dots stay in the text and are only made clear), and the page number at the right, in a column; an index paragraph is cut where a number is followed by the next headword, with a hanging indent. Only plain shapes are recognised (a title, a leader of dots, a page number; or entries that each end in numbers and begin with a capitalised word, in a paragraph dense in numbers), and a printed-page marker that would fall in the middle of an entry moves to the start of it. Separately, a printed-page marker that the page matcher put inside the first word of a paragraph (11,501 of the 460,381 marks, 2.5%: after an opening quote, or a few letters into a drop capital; the data is not changed) is drawn as that paragraph's own break instead of a pill in the middle of the word (`pgmOf` in `readerSection`; `tests/pages.test.js`). In the whole library this touches 9,491 contents paragraphs and 5,391 index paragraphs (about 0.25% of paragraphs, in about 660 texts; 1,194 of them in *Ancient Records of Egypt*); no verse text is touched. Headings that are lines of a contents page (*Conclusion......... Feasts and Offerings*) show the run of dots as one ellipsis. Works in By section, Scroll, Book pages and PDF sheets. Tests: `node tests/lines.test.js` (62).

**Dictionary.** Webster’s Revised Unabridged Dictionary (1913, public domain, Project Gutenberg eBook #29765) is built in and works offline, for the plain words of the old translations (apostleship, tardy, kine, thou, hath, shouldst) that the glossary of religious words does not cover. Each meaning keeps its subject label (Bot., Zoöl.; hover for what it stands for) and its usage label ([Obs.] is shown as “obsolete”). **Left out** (the maker can say if any should come back): word histories (the Gutenberg text lost the Greek and most of the Old English letters), the quotations and their authors’ names, long usage notes and the discussions under “Syn.”. **Care:** 570 entries speak of peoples or faiths in the hostile or mistaken words of 1913 (pagan, heathen, Mohammedan, Hindoo and the like), found by a word list; the box says, once for such a word, that it is shown to be understood, not endorsed, and every box says that it is a book of its time. A copy without the folder says so in the box and works as before; the dictionary is an optional pack (`Lamplighter-dictionary-pack.zip`), and the zips that carry the texts include it. FACT_CHECK.md lists what was found wrong with the data and what was not fixed.

**Keyboard** (press **?** in the reader): f find, n/N next or previous match, c contents, m highlights and notes, b bookmark here, a reading settings, + and - size, t next theme, v go round Scroll, Book pages and PDF sheets, l listen, z full screen, Esc close or clear the search; in pages also the arrows, Space, Page Up and Down, Home and End, [ and ] for the neighbouring sections, and Ctrl+Home and Ctrl+End for the ends of the text. **Light** (Aa panel): a dimming slider and a warm light that takes the blue out for the evening, and an option to keep the screen on while reading (the browser's screen wake lock, where it has one).

### The reader: the whole text, pages, saving

Religion → Library → any text → **Read** opens the reader. **Whole text** (the button in its header, or `?w=1`) turns it from one section at a time into one scroll through the entire text: sections are drawn as you reach them and let go when you are far past, so even the 15 million characters of the Mahabharata scroll as lightly as a short book (about 1,000 page elements at any time). A bar shows **Page N of M** (a page is about 300 words, the same on every screen), a slider and a **Go to page** box move anywhere, and the Contents list jumps within the page. Highlights, notes, bookmarks, threads, find and reading progress all work across sections (a section counts as read when you reach its end after being in it a while, not when you scroll past); your place is remembered.

**Save…** (in the reader's header, and **Save a version…** on the Bibles page) saves the whole text or a whole Bible version: an **e-book (EPUB 3)** for any e-reader or reading app (Kobo, Apple Books, Google Play Books, Android readers, Calibre; a Kindle through Send to Kindle or Calibre), with a contents page and its licence and source; **print or save as a PDF** (a print-ready page with a title page, contents and each section on a new page); **plain text**; **Markdown**; or one **web page**. Optionally your own highlights and notes go with it (highlights keep their colour, notes become numbered notes at the end of the section) and verse numbers can be left out. Everything is built in your browser; nothing is uploaded. The e-book is not yet checked against the official EPUBCheck tool (see FACT_CHECK.md).

**The original edition, and the printed book's pictures.** Every text was made from a public edition (a Project Gutenberg book, a scan on the Internet Archive, a typed reading edition, eBible.org), and if the reader does not suit someone the original is one click away: the reader's details (the contents button in its header) and the Save dialog show **The original edition** with the files the source itself offers and their sizes (for Gutenberg an EPUB with pictures, Kindle, the web page and plain text; for the Internet Archive the PDF of the scan with every picture, an EPUB where there is one, the full text, DjVu). The Lamplighter Project does not download them: each is a plain link to the source's own website, opened by a click, and a link is only ever shown if it is an https address. **Religion → Freedom → Take it with you → The original files of every text** saves a list of every original file by kind (EPUB, Kindle, PDF, plain text, web page) as one web address per line for a download manager (`wget -i lamplighter-original-links.txt`) or as a spreadsheet, with the total size of what was chosen. The data is `texts/originals.js`, made by `python3 -I tools/texts_originals.py` (it asks each source which formats exist and caches the answers in `tools/cache/originals/`; a rerun asks only about texts added since). The texts themselves are plain words, and the builder drops every picture, so the book's own pictures are kept beside them: `python3 -I tools/texts_figures.py` reads each Project Gutenberg text's EPUB with images, finds each illustration and the words printed just before it, finds those words in our text (by their words first; by position between the pictures it could place if that fails; left out if neither) and writes `texts/fig-<id>.js` (the pictures, shrunk to at most 1000 pixels and kept as data addresses so the app still opens from a file) and `texts/figs.js` (which texts have pictures). ### Freedom: promises, your own texts, taking it with you

Religion → **Freedom** has three views, built around one aim: that no one stands between a person and the sources, and that anyone can take their own path.

**Our promises** states what the Lamplighter Project will not do (ask for an account or money, show advertising, track anyone, or send anything anywhere) and how each can be checked. The privacy promise is a test: `node tests/privacy.test.js` reads the whole source and the built page and fails on a network request (the only `fetch` is the offline module's, and only for the site's own four data folders), a socket or beacon, a cookie, a sensor, a tracker, advertising or analytics host, a font, script, style or picture loaded from another site, a new-tab link that does not carry `rel="noopener noreferrer"`, or a web address with a key in it. It also lists how many other sites are named as links to click (77) and checks that none is contacted.

**Your own texts** puts a text of your own into the reader, in any language: drop or choose files (`.txt`, `.md`, `.html`, or an `.epub` e-book, read with the browser's own decompression) or paste words. Markdown and HTML headings become chapters; a plain file is cut into numbered parts; hard-wrapped lines are joined but verse lines, poems and lists keep their breaks; a long chapter is cut into parts at a paragraph so no screen has to lay out a book's worth at once. It then opens like any library text (pages, scroll, find, highlights and notes, read aloud, save as an e-book) and is **kept only in this browser** (an index in the browser's local storage, the words in IndexedDB); it is left out of the Library's lists and counts and of every search, and it is never filtered as "other language". Details (name, author, note) can be edited, and Setup → **Export all** includes your own texts in the backup and Import puts them back. **Any language, either direction.** Every paragraph and heading in the reader carries `dir="auto"`, so Hebrew, Arabic, Persian and Urdu read right to left and "left" alignment means the start of the line; saved e-books and pages do the same. Search matches words in Greek (with every accent and breathing), Cyrillic, Hebrew (with or without the points, and the final letter forms), Arabic (with or without vowel marks, and the alef, yeh and kaf spellings), and the Indian, Armenian, Georgian and Ethiopic scripts. **Search your own texts** with the library, or alone (Search texts → *Only my own texts*); they appear as their own shelf in the results. **Share texts without a server:** on the same page, choose texts and *Save a pack*: one JSON file (optionally with your highlights, notes and bookmarks in those texts, off unless you tick it) to hand on by any means. *Add a pack someone shared* shows what is in it before anything is added, adds each text under an id made from its own words (so a pack cannot overwrite or impersonate another text), never replaces anything, and says plainly if the checksum shows the file was damaged or changed (it shows change, not who made it). `node tests/own.test.js` checks that no word is lost or invented (in English, Hebrew, Arabic and Greek), packs round-trip and hostile packs are cleaned, including a round trip: a text saved by the Lamplighter Project as an EPUB comes back in with every word (and a sample of real library texts does the same).

**Take it with you.** Opened from a file, the Lamplighter Project already works with no internet (the folders beside it are its libraries); this page then says so and gives the ways to keep a copy and to host it yourself (a free static host, a computer on your own network with one Python command, a small computer on a shelf). Opened from a web address (https, or http on the same computer), it registers a service worker (`web/sw.js`) so the page opens with no connection, links a web app manifest and icons so the browser can **install it like an app**, and lists the five libraries with their real sizes (map 3 MB, the Library 1,586 MB, Bibles 66 MB, Find similar 48 MB, the dictionary 21 MB) with **Save** buttons: nothing is saved until you press one, progress is shown, it can be stopped or removed, and the browser is asked to keep what is saved. The service worker only ever talks to its own site, answers only GET, fetches pages from the network first (so updates arrive), and keeps small files you read; the big libraries are saved only on purpose. `node tests/offline.test.js` attacks the manifest rules (only plain files inside the four folders can be fetched: no web addresses, no `..`, no query strings) and checks the service worker file.

**Freedom of conscience: a map and timeline** (Atlas → Stories, `#/atlas/stories/conscience`): twenty-four laws and declarations from the Cyrus Cylinder (539 BCE) and Ashoka's Rock Edict XII to the Universal Declaration (1948), the Indian Constitution and Dignitatis Humanae (1965), shown with their limits and with the reversals (Thessalonica 380, the revocation of the Edict of Nantes 1685). Every claim is qualified where scholars disagree (the Cylinder is not simply a human-rights charter; there was probably no single "Edict of Milan"; Warsaw's freedom was the nobles'; Locke left out atheists; Maryland's act could punish by death). The checking record is in `FACT_CHECK.md`.

**The freedom documents, whole** (Library → *Freedom of conscience*, or Religion → Freedom → *Read the freedom documents*): ten texts to read, search and mark like any other. The Cyrus Cylinder (Robert William Rogers's translation of 1912, from an older revision of its Wikisource page), the Edicts of Asoka (Vincent A. Smith's translation of 1909, OCR text of a scan at the Internet Archive), Roger Williams's *The Bloudy Tenent of Persecution* (1644, in the 1848 edition), Spinoza's *Theological-Political Treatise* (1670, R. H. M. **The quality check.** Before a passage is shown it is read once more (`psuGate`, `src/js/2z-passage-core.js`). Each of these is a reason to pass a pick over, and the draw goes on to the next pick of the same stream, so the same seed still gives the same passage: a share of its words that are not words of the Library's own typed texts (the long s read as an f, "combnft", "confefled"; the list is `src/js/2z-passage-words.js`, made by `tools/passage_words.js` from the Library, nothing fetched); stray marks; a running head or a page number in the text; a footnote letter stuck to the first word; a start in the middle of a sentence; text that is not sentence-like; a list of names or things; mostly capitalised names; a note on places; the voice and citations of an editor. A run of verses is judged only as a list of names. A book asked for by name is still drawn from (its best pick is given). Italics marks in a text (`_who_`) are taken off. **A scan's own mistakes** (the maker found "z¢s earth … out of ove thing" in *The Hermetic Museum*): a symbol that is not punctuation in or against a word (¢ £ § | ~ ^ and the like; a pound sign before a figure, a section sign before a number and a translator's square brackets, as in Waite's "[a] spirit", are not strays) passes a passage over, and so does a word that is no word of the Library and is exactly one machine confusion from one of 109 very common words (a letter mistaken for another: b for h, c for e, u or v for n; or a few run together: li for h, tb for th, rn for m, ii for u: "ove" is "one", "tbe" and "thc" and "tlie" are "the", "aud" is "and"). A plain edit of one letter would not do: "ove" has a dozen neighbours in the word list. Rare real words have no such reading, and "mau", "ber" and "vor" are left alone; the rule is asked only of a scan (measured: 143 words in 4.2 million in 70 scans, nearly all real misreadings; 5 in 3.8 million words of typed books, all the Latin "bis"; stray symbols: about one word in a thousand in a scan, none in the typed books). The text is never mended or hidden: a book asked for by name still shows its best pick. **Every card from a machine-read scan** (the Library's own test, `provKind`) says so under the words, in the same words each time: "Machine-read from a scan: some words may be wrong. Check the page scan." with the link to a search for the passage inside the scan on the Internet Archive where the Library has one (a book scanned in several parts links each part).

**A passage and a page are different things, and the switch says which is drawn: Passage, One page or Two pages.** A **passage** (the default) is cut by structure, a unit the text itself marks off, with an exact reference, and it is always clean (below). A **page** is the page of the book as it is printed, with **everything** on it, an editor's note or a damaged line too, cited by its printed number ("p. 214", "pp. 214–215", "Vol. 2, p. 33"). A scanned text keeps where each printed page begins (`texts/pg-<id>.js`, `2z-pages-core.js`; 991 of the 1,023 scans have them), so a page runs from one mark to the next and every page that may be drawn is equally likely (`psgPickLeaf`, `psuLeaves`, `psuLeafText` in `2z-passage-core.js`); a text with no page marks (a typed text) is cut into a window of about a page (2,200 characters, 4,400 for two) from paragraph edge to paragraph edge and cited by its section. Three choices say what a page may be (they show for a page, are kept for next time and are kept in the address as `pf=`): **Skip front matter** (on: the pages numbered in roman numerals, i, ii, iii, and in a text with no page numbers a preface or an introduction), **Skip glossaries, appendixes and indexes** (on) and **Clean text only** (off: the quality check below, without its demand that a page begin a sentence). A page has no **Read on** (the book goes on from **In context**). A draw code of a page is `<id>.page.<seed>`, of two `<id>.pages.<seed>`; codes made before (`short`, `medium`, `long`, `longer`) replay as they were drawn. A reference is **never invented**: a verse number is shown only where the text has one, a printed page only where the book's own page numbers were found, and the card says what kind of address it has.
* *A text that numbers its verses* (the 87 Bible, Apocrypha and Septuagint books, which carry `[[n]]` markers; `src/js/2z-passage-core.js`): a run of whole verses from the edge of a sentence to the edge of a sentence, at the translators' own paragraph breaks where it can (140 to 1,100 characters, 500 at the median), cited "Psalm 4:1–3" or "Genesis 6:5–8"; a title line ("A Psalm by David.") comes with verse 1, a "(BETH)" line with the verse after it, a speech is not cut open where a whole one fits, and a chapter that begins in the middle of a sentence is not a place to begin. A page of such a book is the same kind of unit at about 1,500 characters. A chapter whose verse numbers do not run in order is read as prose and cited by its chapter.
* *A text whose paragraphs are numbered in order within a section* (the Dhammapada, the Tirukkural, the Poetic Edda and some thirty more): a paragraph or a few, cited by the text's own numbers ("Dhammapada, Chapter XVIII. Impurity, nos. 235–238"). A restart, a gap or a misread digit in the run and no number is taken.
* *Any other text*: a paragraph or a few, begun where a paragraph begins, cited by the book's title, its section (the heading made readable: capitals read as a title, "part 2 of 5" and the builder's first-words tail taken off; "section 3 of 9" where the section has no name of its own; "part 3 of 9" for a later part of a long section) and, for the 1,078 texts whose printed page numbers were found (`texts/pg-<id>.js`, as in the reader), the printed page: "Travels in Arabia Deserta, Volume 1, p. 477".

Editors' notes, cross-references, citations, indexes, glossaries, bibliographies, contents lists, lists of names and numbers, running page heads and scan garbage are left out, and so are the front and back matter of a book (an index, notes, a short introduction; for long scanned editions, the first and last tenth of the parts); a typed text's footnotes, printed after its verses as an ascending run of numbers (Rodwell's Qur'an), are recognised and left out, and the note numbers stuck to its words are taken off. A scanned book is held to a stricter standard: a pick with scan damage (marks, stray letters, digits glued to words, capitals inside words, more than 1.5% of its words) is passed over and the draw goes on, and a book whose every pick is worse than twice that is left out of a draw among many books (a book chosen by name still gives its cleanest). A scan's "¬" hyphens are joined. The place of a draw is chosen as before: every shelf of the places you chose equally likely, then every book on it, then every character of text that can be part of a passage; the unit is a fixed function of the place, so a draw code replays it. **Draw from** is the quick choices and the sections above (the one select of the first release, with its Everything, one shelf, one tradition of the Journey, chosen areas and one book, is every one of those choices still: Everything is a quick choice, shelves and chosen areas are under Shelves, the Journey's nineteen traditions are under Religions → From the Journey, one book is under Books and on every card as Another from this book); a link from a book or a tradition page names its book or tradition. **How long a passage is** (Passage only): **Short** (a verse or two, or a few lines: 40 to 420 characters of verses, a paragraph of about 280 in prose), **Medium** (a few verses or a paragraph: what a passage always was, 140 to 1,100 and about 560) and **Longer** (a longer run on to the next natural break: 500 to 2,600 and about 1,100); the choice is kept (`passage.prefs.len`) and may be named in an address (`?len=short|medium|longer`, or `s`, `m`, `l`). Draw codes: `b-psa.passage.…` is a passage (Medium), `b-psa.passage-short.…` and `b-psa.passage-longer.…` say the length they were drawn at, `b-gen.long.…` a page, and the four old lengths (`short`, `medium`, `longer`) still replay as they were drawn.

**Ways to make the seed: Method** (21 November 2026, stage 2 of `RANDOM_SUITE.md`; the maker: "the more filters and clean methods for randomization the better. it makes the path less daunting if you can just explore freely"). A closed section of the controls, **Method**, beside How to draw, with five ways (one panel at a time): **Chance** (what there always was: Another, Space and N, the secure random source with the times of your own key presses mixed in; unchanged, still one press, and still the default); **A text** (a word, a name or a question); **A date** (any date); **Dice** (a d6, d10 or d20, rolled by the secure generator or typed in from your own real dice); and **Today** (the daily draw of a saved filter marked Daily, listed here as a fifth way and still where it was: "Show today's" on the saved filter). Each makes the same kind of seed, 128 bits as 32 hexadecimal characters, so the card, its receipt and its draw code work as before; only how the seed is made differs, and Another after any of them is a draw by chance again. The pure arithmetic is in `src/js/2z-passage-core.js` (`psm*`), the section in `src/js/6d2-passage-filters.js` (`psxMethodHTML`, `psxMethodPatch`), the wiring in `src/js/6d-passage.js` (`draw({ method })`, `psgStreamOf`), styles under `.psgx-mx*`.
- **A text.** The seed is SHA-256 of `lamplighter-text|` and the text read as Unicode NFKC, lower case, spaces collapsed, so the same text with the same choices gives the same passage on any device. The text is read once, to make the seed: it is not kept, sent, put in the address, the history or the card (the card never repeats a typed question), and the address carries the draw code, whose last part is the seed, with `meth=text` so a reload or a friend's link keeps the receipt's words. The receipt says that it was drawn from a text used as a seed, that the same text gives the same passage and that the meaning of the words played no part.
- **A date.** The same, from `lamplighter-date|YYYY-MM-DD` (only a real day of the calendar is read). The date is not kept or put in the address either (`meth=date`), and the page says nothing of a date's significance: the receipt says only that the digits of the date played a part.
- **Dice.** The books the sections leave are put in a fixed list (shelf by shelf, then in the Library's order, as the Books section shows them: `psmOrder`). The first dice, as many as it takes to pass the end of the list (three d6 or d10 for 197 books, two d20), are read as one number in base 6, 10 or 20 (a d10's 10 counts as 0); that number divided by the length of the list leaves the book's place on it, and the numbers at the very top, which would make the first books likelier, are thrown out and rolled again (the step the secure draw uses: over every reading of the dice every book is named exactly as often as every other, tested). Two more dice say how far through the book the place is (two d10 are a percentile): `psgBelow` takes its first place from the dice (`rng.at`, only a dice seed has one) and the rest from the seed's own stream, so a stretch that cannot be used, a note or a list, is passed over as always. The seed of a dice draw **holds the numbers** (`d1ce`, the die, how many name the book, those, the place), so the draw code carries them, any device reads them back, and the receipt shows every number, anything thrown out, and the mapping in three lines on the page. With dice every book on the list has the same chance, so a big shelf comes up more often than by chance, where every shelf has the same chance first; the page says so. Dice read the books the sections leave and ignore the weights of a saved mix.
- **Neutral.** What the card says of a way is the same for every draw of it and names the rule (`psmHow`); no "your passage", no "answer" for a typed question, nothing about astrology or numerology, and the introduction to the section says that a passage met this way is a place to start reading, and that is all. The history of drawing lots from books is not told here: it needs sources opened first.
- **Not changed:** the draw code format (`<book>.<kind>.<seed>`, every old code replays: a seed that is not dice picks exactly as it did), the receipt of a draw by chance, the keys, the address keys. The daily draw's receipt now says what it is (today's date and the filter's name as the seed): it used to say "secure random", which it was not.

### The voice: guided practices and reading aloud (18 November 2026)
The maker found the robotic voice in the guided practices distracting (“some of these AI just sound so chopped”), and the reader's read-aloud used the same speech. What was wrong, and what is done, in the order the maker would meet it.

**Text first.** A guided practice (Firelight and the others under Worship → Ways to worship, and Meditate) shows its guidance as text. A voice is something you add: one switch, **Add a voice**, in the set-up sheet of every practice and on the Meditate page's defaults card, off until you turn it on and remembered after (`meditation.prefs.voiceOn`). **Bells only** is still the other choice. The old default was voice and text together, saved as `guide: 'voice'` by anyone who never changed it; that now reads as text with the switch off (`lsGuideMode`). The words always show, and the voice reads them.

**Which voice.** The cause of the robotic voice was the choice of voice. The old picker gave 50 points for a liked name and 40 for “Premium”, so a plain Samantha (56) beat the Premium and Enhanced voices installed on the maker's Mac (Matilda and Zoe, Premium; Evan, Enhanced: 48). The ranking (`lsVoiceTier`, `lsVoiceScore` in `src/js/2z-listen-core.js`) now sorts what the browser lists into: **best** (a name with Premium, Enhanced, Natural, Neural or Siri), **good** (plain voices people like: Samantha, Daniel, Karen, Moira, Tessa, Alex, Kate…), other English voices, **older style and novelty** voices (Fred, Zarvox, Whisper, the Eloquence family Eddy, Flo, Grandma, Grandpa, Reed, Rocko, Sandy, Shelley) last, then other languages. A voice **on the device always ranks above one that is online** (an online voice sends its words to its maker's service to be spoken, which the app never does by itself; it sits in its own group, labelled), and the voice the computer is set to speak with is a small plus within its kind. **Automatic** is the top of the list. This is a guess from names, and the list says so by showing the groups. The **voice chooser** (`src/js/32-voice-chooser.js`: a list in groups, a **Preview** button, the pick saved as `speech.voice`) is one piece of page used in the set-up sheet, on the Meditate card and in the reader's read-aloud panel, and they share the pick (the first time, whatever was chosen in the reader or in Meditate before is carried over). It lists English voices, and every voice only when the device has none.

**Pacing.** One engine, `Speech` in `src/js/31-bell-speech.js`, speaks for the guided practices, the reader and the “Read this aloud” button of Worship together. It never hands a whole cue or paragraph to the browser as one long utterance (some browsers cut one off after about fifteen seconds). It says it **sentence by sentence** (`lsPieces`: pieces of at most 240 characters, 150 for an online voice, cut at sentence ends and only then at a comma) with a **real silence** after each, which the voices do not leave: in a guided practice 0.8 s after a sentence, 1.4 s after a breath marked “…”, 1.5 s at the end of a cue; in a reading 0.42 s and 1.0 s after a paragraph; a clause cut out of a long sentence waits only 0.15 s so it does not sound chopped; every pause is divided by the speed (longer when slower). A very short sentence is joined to the next in a reading (“Amen.”) but stands on its own in a guided practice (“May I be safe.”). The default speed is **0.85×** for both (the reader's list is 0.7× to 2×), the pitch 0.95 where the engine honours it (not every engine does). Every cue of every practice and length is checked to be said, pauses included, before the next one (`tests/voice.test.js`; the last cue of Ground was moved 4 s earlier for it).

**Stop and resume cannot leave it stuck.** Pause is not the browser's own pause (which on several systems never resumes, and can leave the voice swallowing the next thing said): it stops the sentence at once and says it again from its start on resume, and a pause in the silence after a sentence goes on with the next. `cancel()` always leaves the engine clean, sets going a browser left paused, and waits the moment browsers need before the next utterance. A late “ended” from a stopped utterance is ignored (a generation count). A watchdog gives up on a voice that goes quiet without saying so, or never ends; a sentence the system stopped from outside (seen once in a real browser) is said again once; a piece that fails is skipped; three in a row end it with a message (“The voice did not respond, so the guidance stays as text”, and in a sit the voice is turned off for the rest of it); a browser that blocks speech until the page has been clicked is told apart (“blocked”). Late cues never pile up (the queue holds at most five).

**The note on adding a better voice** (`lsAdvice`, `lsHowTo`): shown, under the chooser, only when the best voice found is a plain one, as a short “Want a smoother voice?” that opens to one line each for Mac (System Settings → Accessibility → Spoken Content → System Voice → Manage Voices…, a voice marked Premium or Enhanced), Windows, Android and, on an iPhone or iPad, iOS, the person's own system first. With a Premium voice on the device there is no note (the maker's Mac is one: the chooser starts on Matilda (Premium), the computer's own voice).

**What was checked, and what cannot be.** The logic by `tests/voice.test.js` (a fake speech synthesis and a fake clock). In the Browser pane on a Mac with 183 system voices, with the sound turned to zero so nothing played but every event fired: the list and groups, the pick saved and shared between the sheet, the card and the reader, a Firelight sit with the voice on (the voice used, 0.85×, a gap of 0.94 s between sentences, pause stops it at once and resume says the sentence again, End early leaves the engine idle), and the reader reading the Dhammapada (the voice, the 1.18 s paragraph gap, the marked sentence, pause, next, a change of voice and of speed heard from the next sentence, close). **Not checked:** how any voice sounds (nobody listened), whether the lower pitch is audible, and anything on Windows, Android, iOS, Safari or Firefox (the menu paths in the note are the systems' own, written down, not followed on each).

**Not built.** Pre-recorded narration of the guided practices from an openly licensed neural voice, made when the app is built, which would sound better than any system voice and the same on every device. There are 506 distinct sentences in all the practices (about 31 minutes at 0.85×, roughly 5 MB at 24 kbit/s), and only six carry a word the person chooses. It needs a licence check of the voice and a go-ahead from the maker before anything is fetched; the engine already speaks one sentence at a time, found by its text, so a recorded sentence could be played in place of a system voice with a small addition (a lookup in `speakPiece`).

Files: `src/js/2z-listen-core.js` (all the decisions, pure), `31-bell-speech.js` (the engine), `32-voice-chooser.js`, `68-meditate.js` (the switch), `6kl-listen.js` (the reader's player), `src/css/app.css` (`.vchoose`, `.vc-*`); tests `tests/voice.test.js`, `tests/listen.test.js`, `tests/meditation.test.js`.

### High-control groups and contested movements (17 November 2026)
Traditions → **High-control groups** (`#/traditions/movements`; `src/js/2z-data-movements.js`, `6zy-movements.js`, `tests/movements.test.js`). The newer religious movements are the Atlas’s biggest hole: their scriptures are post-1930 and in copyright, so the Library cannot hold them. This is how the Lamplighter Project covers them honestly: what each movement is and did, every claim graded for its source, and pointers to where its own texts can be read, never the texts. The maker asked for “a section and database for the ones that are clearly cults like Scientology”; the section is named for what it records (“high-control groups and contested movements”), and the page says in its own words what that term means, why “cult” is not used as a verdict, and what a few cited yardsticks are (Lifton’s eight themes of 1961, the ICSA’s checklist, Lalich’s four dimensions of 2004), with the caution that a movement can meet some and not others and that these are lenses, not scores. The first set is **13 movements** (Scientology, Peoples Temple, Heaven’s Gate, Aum Shinrikyo, the Branch Davidians, the Order of the Solar Temple, NXIVM, the Family International, the Rajneesh movement, the Unification Church and Raëlism, which are the two graded *Contested*, the Twelve Tribes and the FLDS), one card each with nine labelled parts: at a glance (founded and how the date is known, now, size); what the group says about itself; what independent sources document; legal outcomes with dates; the group’s response; harm that is documented (deaths, violence, sexual abuse, harm to children, financial exploitation, family separation, unpaid labour, health), stated plainly; its texts (title, year, publisher, rights, and whether anything is readable here, which almost nothing is; the books wanted are in `POST1930_WANTED.csv`); how sure we are; and sources and where to look further. **The grades** say what a claim rests on, never how bad it is: *court or official record*, *independent reporting* (at least two independent sources), *former members’ accounts* (graded lower, with whose they are), *alleged* (and by whom) and *the group’s own statement*. A claim about a living person rests on a court record, an official report, or at least two independent sources, or it says “alleged” and by whom. No scripture and no recruiting material is reproduced, and a word of accusation is never used outside quotation marks (tests). The page filters by documented harm, by whether the movement is active, changed or ended, and by how settled the record is, searches by name, and lists seven groups “not yet written up” with the reason. **Every card still says it is under review, and none is marked checked**, but the claims were read against sources on 22 November 2026, in two passes: the first read 147 of the 156 claims (98 found in the page read, 37 in part, 12 not in the page read; 60 corrected or narrowed; 9 not read), and the second read the 24 lines the first could not settle, so that all 156 claims have now been read: 111 found in the page read, 43 in part and 2 not in the page read, and 74 corrected or narrowed. **Questions to ask any group (18 November 2026, BACKLOG D9).** The maker, of this page: “is good. ### Find similar passages
From any passage to the passages of other books and traditions that read most like it: a random passage (**Find similar passages** under it), words selected in the reader (**Similar** in the selection bar), or a few sentences you type about a story or an idea (Religion → Connections → **Find similar**). Results are grouped by tradition, closest first, each with the words the two passages share ("flood", "ark ↔ ship"), a **side by side** view, and the passage in the reader. A passage's own tradition is left out unless you ask for it. Where scholars have set two books beside each other (the curated Parallel passages), the note says what the evidence supports; otherwise the page says a match only shows that two passages read alike, not that one borrowed from the other.

How it works, plainly: every English text in the library (the scriptures; see the end of this section for what is left out) is cut into about 220,000 passages of a paragraph or so; each becomes a list of weighted words (TF-IDF, so a rare word like *raven* counts for more than *the*); a statistical step, latent semantic analysis, finds the directions in which words and passages vary together and gives each passage 64 numbers, so *ark*, *ship* and *boat* land close together. A search scores the passage against all the others two ways, closeness in that space and exact overlap of rare words, and blends the two. It is classic statistics computed once offline, with no neural network, no server and nothing leaving the machine. It finds likeness in vocabulary and subject; it does not understand meaning, so some matches are shallow (the same few common words), and it is weakest where translators use quite different words for the same thing. **How well it works, measured.** `node tools/similar_bench.js` asks the matcher 76 questions taken from the curated Parallel passages (a passage from one book; do the scholars' matching passages of the other book come back, with the curated pairs themselves not used as a hint). Today the partner book is among the sections the page shows (a tradition’s two best, and up to two more from other books when they are within 15% of the best match of all) about 25% of the time (19 of 76, on 16 November 2026; the extra sections made no difference to that count), and among the ten best sections overall about 18% (14 of 76), against a chance rate near 0.1%. These figures move by a few questions whenever texts are added or taken out, because more passages compete with the curated partner for the same places (the Church Fathers, tried on 16 November, halved the ten-best figure), so a change to what is indexed is judged by building the index both ways. Many of those pairs are thematic (a psalm and an Egyptian hymn) with little shared vocabulary, which is where word statistics are weakest. Tuning the weights, the number of dimensions, the number of stored words, and adding feedback expansion all landed within noise of each other (feedback made it worse), so the settings were left where they were; the motifs gave the one small, consistent gain. A neural sentence-embedding model would likely do better on exactly those thematic pairs, at the cost of a model download and a long one-time computation.

The index lives in a `similar/` folder next to Lamplighter.html (about 66 MB in seven files, loaded the first time it is used) and is rebuilt from the texts with `node tools/build_similar.js` (about three minutes). If a text changes after the index was built, that text is left out of results until the index is rebuilt. Which texts go in is decided by the Library group (since 17 November 2026; the first letter of an id no longer says what a book is): every group that holds a tradition's own writings is indexed, a shelf-only group (study aids, occult, Masonic, field records, folklore, folk tales, the imagined shelf) never is, and the two mixed groups, `ec` and `mo`, take only the ids listed in `tools/similar_include.json`; `tools/similar_skip.json` lists books to leave out, with the reason for each. The index holds 445 texts and 319,867 passages (24 November 2026; 419 and 296,059 on 17 November, 326 and 228,869 before). No file may reach the 25 MiB a free static host allows: since 24 November the passage vectors are in two files (`chunks-2.js`, `chunks-4.js`) and the largest file is 16.5 MiB, so about 480,000 passages fit before the fattest one passes it (the build exits with an error if any file does). Of the 35 texts added in rounds 24 to 26, 26 are in and nine are left out, each with its reason in `tools/similar_skip.json` (the numbers are in `tools/README.md`, "Find similar after rounds 24 to 26"). `node tools/build_similar.js --stats --per-text` counts passages per book without building, and `node tools/similar_bench.js --crowd ids.json --by-book` shows which books take the ten best places (`--leave ids.json` asks as if books were not in the index, `--show <set>` prints the groups the page would show). Not indexed, so that scriptures are compared with scriptures: the modern scholarship in the folder (`z-`), the Theosophical *Secret Doctrine* and the *Kybalion*, the freedom-of-conscience documents (`f-`: they quote the Bible so closely that their quotations crowded out the real parallels), and the occult, Masonic, Spiritualist and field-record shelves (`h- m- v- k- a-`). Tried and kept out on 16 November 2026: with those 94 books in (424 texts, 332,000 passages, 69 MB), 179 of the 760 best-ten places over the 76 benchmark questions went to them and 65 of the 76 questions had at least one, while the ten-best and mean-reciprocal-rank figures fell (13 to 12 of 76; 0.084 to 0.071). The field records, which retell tribal myths, are the shelf most worth reconsidering (see `FACT_CHECK.md`). Also left out, each with its reason in `tools/similar_skip.json`: the 33 Church Fathers volumes (with them in they took 40% of the benchmark's best-ten places and halved its ten-best figure), Rodkinson’s modern *History of the Talmud* (`e-b14`; the thirteen tractate entries `e-b01` to `e-b13` are in, now that each tractate is cut out of its scan once), and every extra translation of a work the index already holds in another (the Library's own Qur'an is indexed, not Palmer's, Pickthall's or Sale's); `node tools/similar_dups.js` lists candidates.

### Search, jumping around, and common ground

* **Search every text** (Library → Search texts, or the box at the top of Religion). A full-text search over all 1,537 readable texts in English: it ignores case, accents, punctuation and verse numbers; treats variant spellings of a name as one (Jehovah and Yahweh, Gilgamish and Gilgamesh, honour and honor; switch it off if you want); takes "exact phrases", `OR` or `|` between alternatives and `-word` to leave one out; finds words in the same paragraph or, looser, the same section; can be limited to one collection or to the texts you have started. Results stream in as the texts are read (the whole library, 676 MB, takes several seconds on the computer the tests run on), are grouped by text in chronological order within each collection, and can be filtered by collection, sorted by number of passages, opened in the reader at the exact paragraph, copied with a citation, or **selected from several traditions and made into a thread**. There is no index to build or go stale: texts are loaded four at a time, scanned and let go. **The results page leads with the results** (UX-06): the query box, one quiet button *Filters and search tips* (shut unless a filter is on; it holds the tips on quotes, OR and the minus sign, the scope, the options and Leave areas out), the progress line, then the first twenty passages and a *More* button for twenty more (the page was 40,792 px high with every text’s cards in it; it is now 4,220 px at 1280 × 800 and 7,383 px at 375 × 812); on a phone the collections are one row to swipe. Every address (`?q=`, `c`, `m`, `w`, `e`, `a`) works as it did. **The search of every text lets each text go, and asks first on a website (21 November 2026, AA-01 of `ALPHA_AUDIT.md`, a blocker).** Taking a `<script>` out of the page again does not free a text: the browser keeps the script’s source and every string in it, and a search of every text took the page to about 4.4 GB. `TXT.scan` (`6c-texts.js`) looks at a text and lets it go: on a web address (and localhost) the file is fetched and read as data (`txtParse` in `2z-source-core.js`: a text file is JSON-compatible, written by `json.dumps`), so nothing holds it once it has been searched; from a file, which cannot fetch its neighbours, and for a text of your own or a book, it is still loaded as a script and unloaded; a text that was already open stays open. What is kept of a hit is a copy of its own words, not a piece of the section it was cut from (`srchOwn`). Over the whole library on a local server the heap stays under about 250 MB (the page was at 4.4 GB) and the search takes about 5.7 s here (the audit measured 2.6 to 4.3 s with scripts); the results and the counts are the same. On a website (`copyWhere()` says `web`: not a file, not localhost) the scope “All texts” first says what it would download (about 1.6 GB) and offers “Search this shelf” (the shelf of the book the search began in, else The Bible) before “Search everything”; after a yes the question is not asked again in that visit, and no other scope ever asks. `tests/alpha-fixes-a.test.js` measures what is still held after a search of the 24 biggest texts (under 12 MB, measured after a full garbage collection; the script way kept 350 MB).
* **Search and jump** (press `/` or Ctrl/⌘-K anywhere). One box that finds a text, station, tradition, word, parallel passage, thread or page by name, and, as its first row, searches every text for what you typed. `←` and `→` move between sections in the reader; select words in the reader and choose *Search* to find where else they appear; `?` lists the shortcuts.
* **Common ground** (Connections, the first tab). The case that there is much less to fight over than it seems, built only from what the library shows and saying where it stops. Every number is computed from the classified echoes and the traditions each station belongs to: that all 19 traditions are linked to each other, how few steps separate any two, and an interactive ring showing who is connected to whom and how (documented borrowing, shared ancestry, contact, independent parallel, contested). It splits the overlap honestly into **shared history** and **independent convergence** (and says how much is merely disputed), gathers the passages that say the same thing in several traditions (the reciprocity rule, answering harm, peace, the stranger, the poor, one's own faults, impermanence, the divine within, many paths to one God), counts a dozen shared concerns (compassion, peace, truth, forgiveness, the stranger…) across the whole library as a live word map, and says plainly where traditions really differ. It does not claim they are the same: overlap is not sameness, and a case that hid the differences would be easy to dismiss. A seventh Gather template, *One teaching, many voices*, turns it into an interfaith gathering around the reciprocity rule read from four traditions.

## The text library

These are older translations and some readings have been superseded. The famous Sumerian myths of Inanna’s descent and Enheduanna’s hymns exist only in modern copyrighted translations (ETCSL’s are not openly licensed), so the Library points to them without copying them; the Nag Hammadi library and the Dead Sea Scrolls are in the same position.

What is not here, and why: the Dead Sea Scrolls (the only entry with a text is the Damascus Document, from the 1910 Cairo Genizah edition), the Nag Hammadi library, most of the Mishnah, the Zohar and the Hadith have no public-domain English translation to copy; the Talmud is here only as Rodkinson’s 1896–1903 translation of twenty-four tractates (some of them only the Mishnah, all in a free idiom) and a short anthology from a 1901 book, and the Zohar entry is an anthology. Of the Puranas the Library has the Vishnu, the whole Bhagavata (Subba Rau’s 1928 prose, beside Sinha’s 1901 study, which is not a translation), the Devī Bhāgavata, Mārkaṇḍeya, Matsya, Agni, Garuda and the first half of the Brahma-Vaivarta; no open English was found for the other ten or so. About 458 entries come from scans, so they can contain OCR errors, editors’ introductions and notes (each text’s page says so). Items first published in 1927–1929 (for example Evans-Wentz’s Tibetan Book of the Dead and Milarepa, Dubs’s Xunzi, Mei’s Mozi) are public domain in the United States; if you share the folder, check the rules where you live. Each text’s page names its translator, edition, licence and source, and `texts/LICENSES.txt` lists them all.

**Checked against what it says it is.** `tools/audit_texts.py` reads the whole folder against the Library and the Atlas catalogue and writes `../TEXT_AUDIT.md` (what each file really holds, scan quality, structure checks, who translated what, what is missing) and `../TEXT_GAPS.csv` (every Atlas text record and whether a text covers it). The first audit found the Heart and Diamond Sūtra entries held the wrong book, the Upanishads entry held three of the twelve it promised, and several volumes were filed under two names; those are fixed. Twelve of Max Müller’s Upanishad translations, the Larger and Smaller Sukhāvatī-vyūha, the Amitāyur-dhyāna-sūtra and Cowell’s *Buddhacarita* are now their own entries, and the Acaranga and Kalpa Sūtras, the Odes and Psalms of Solomon, and the Great Learning with the Doctrine of the Mean and Mencius are cut into separate entries. A Library title now says what the file holds (the Vishnu Purana is not “the Puranas”). `tools/atlas_text_aliases.json` is the hand-checked list that links Atlas text records to Library entries.
